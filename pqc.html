Part 1 · Foundations

Why quantum computers threaten today's encryption, and what PQC is.

1.1 Background: what you need first

This course is about protecting data from quantum computers. Before we start, here are the few ideas it builds on, each in plain words with a small example.

Locks and keys

Cryptography is the science of keeping messages secret and genuine. It comes in two kinds.

SymmetricOne shared secret key both locks and unlocks. Fast, used for the bulk of the data. Example: AES.
Public-keyEach person has a public key (an open padlock anyone may use) and a private key (the only key that opens it). It lets strangers set up a shared key and sign messages. Examples: RSA, Diffie–Hellman.
RSA in one line. Multiplying is easy: 3 × 11 = 33. Going backward, "which two primes make 33?", is factoring. For 33 it is instant, but for a number with 600 digits no ordinary computer can do it. RSA publishes the big product and keeps the two primes secret.

Hash functions: fingerprints of data

A hash function turns any input into a short fixed-size "fingerprint". It is easy to compute and practically impossible to reverse, and a tiny change in the input changes the whole output.

SHA-256("hello") = 2cf24dba5fb0a30e…

SHA-256("hellp") = fdd7585e08c4e2af…

One letter changed, a completely different fingerprint.

Clock arithmetic and XOR

mod means "the remainder after dividing", like a clock that wraps around. On a 12-hour clock, 14 o'clock is 2 o'clock: 14 mod 12 = 2.

Example. 17 mod 5: walk 17 steps around a 5-hour clock. Three full turns use 15 steps, 2 are left, so 17 mod 5 = 2.

XOR (written ⊕) compares two bits: the answer is 1 when they differ and 0 when they match. It is how bits are "added on a 2-hour clock".

aba ⊕ b

Quantum computers in brief

An ordinary computer stores bits, each 0 or 1, like a coin lying heads or tails. A quantum computer stores qubits, which can be in a blend of 0 and 1 at once (a superposition), like a spinning coin.

This does not make it faster at everything. It is faster only for problems where a clever quantum algorithm can make the wrong answers cancel out. Two such algorithms matter for security: Shor's and Grover's.

Shor's algorithm

Shor's algorithm finds the period of powers on a clock: how often the pattern repeats. Knowing the period cracks factoring, and also the discrete logarithm problem behind Diffie–Hellman (explained in 1.2). Here is the classical half of the idea with N = 15.

2⁰→12¹→22²→42³→82⁴→12⁵→22⁶→42⁷→8

Powers of 2 mod 15 repeat every 4 steps (same colours), so the period is r = 4.

Take 2^(r/2) = 2² = 4. Then 4 − 1 = 3 and 4 + 1 = 5, and indeed 15 = 3 × 5.

For a 600-digit number the pattern is far too long to list, but a large quantum computer finds the period quickly. That breaks RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography.

Grover's algorithm

Grover's algorithm searches a list of N possibilities in about √N steps instead of N. Against a secret key this halves the key's strength in bits.

KeyOrdinary guessingWith Grover
AES-1282¹²⁸ tries√2¹²⁸ = 2⁶⁴ steps (too weak)
AES-2562²⁵⁶ tries2¹²⁸ steps (still safe)
The fix for Grover is easy: double the key length. The fix for Shor is not: public-key cryptography needs entirely new maths. That is what this course is about.

Harvest now, decrypt later

An attacker can record encrypted traffic today, store it, and decrypt it years from now once a big quantum computer exists. So data that must stay secret for a long time is already at risk, even though no such computer exists yet.

1.2 The quantum threat: what breaks and when

Almost every secure website, bank app and messaging service relies on two kinds of public-key maths. Shor's algorithm (1.1) breaks both.

Factoring (RSA)

RSA's security rests on factoring being hard (1.1). Shor's algorithm finds the factors quickly, so a large enough quantum computer breaks RSA outright.

Discrete logarithms (Diffie–Hellman, ECC)

The other big family uses powers on a clock (mod, 1.1). Going forward is easy: 3⁴ mod 17 = 81 mod 17 = 13. Going backward ("which power of 3 gives 13?") is the discrete logarithm problem, and it is very hard for big numbers.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange, step by step

This is how two strangers agree on a secret key in public. Public numbers: clock size p = 23 and base g = 5.

Alice secretly picks a = 6 and sends A = 5⁶ mod 23 = 8.

Bob secretly picks b = 15 and sends B = 5¹⁵ mod 23 = 19.

Alice computes B⁶ = 19⁶ mod 23 = 2. Bob computes A¹⁵ = 8¹⁵ mod 23 = 2. Both have the shared secret 2.

Eve sees 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret she must find a = 6 from 5ᵃ mod 23 = 8: a discrete logarithm.

Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) plays the same game, but the "clock" is replaced by points on a curve (shapes like y² = x³ + ax + b), which gives the same security with much smaller keys. Shor's algorithm solves discrete logarithms too, so Diffie–Hellman and ECC fall as well.

What survives

ToolUsed forAgainst a quantum computer
RSAKey exchange, signaturesBroken (Shor)
Diffie–Hellman, ECC (ECDH, ECDSA)Key exchange, signaturesBroken (Shor)
AES (symmetric)Encrypting dataWeakened by Grover (1.1): use 256-bit keys
SHA-2, SHA-3 (hash functions)Fingerprints of dataWeakened slightly: use longer outputs

So the urgent job is to replace public-key cryptography: key exchange and digital signatures.

Mosca's rule: why act now

Michele Mosca gave a simple test. Let

xHow many years your data must stay secret.
yHow many years it takes to switch to new cryptography.
zHow many years until a quantum computer can break today's locks.

If x + y > z, you are already too late: secrets recorded today ("harvest now, decrypt later", 1.1) will be readable while they still matter.

Example. Medical records must stay private for x = 25 years, and switching takes y = 5 years. If a quantum computer arrives in z = 15 years, then 25 + 5 = 30 > 15: the switch should have started long ago.

1.3 PQC basics: new hard problems

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) means public-key methods that run on ordinary computers but rest on maths problems that are believed to be hard even for quantum computers. No special hardware is needed: it is just new software.

Two jobs to replace

Key exchange: a KEMA key encapsulation mechanism lets two parties agree on a shared secret key (then used with AES). It replaces Diffie–Hellman and RSA key exchange.
Digital signaturesA signature proves who wrote a message and that it wasn't changed, like a tamper-proof wax seal. It replaces RSA and ECDSA signatures.

How a KEM works

The three steps are called KeyGen (Bob makes a key pair), Encaps (Alice makes a secret and its capsule, the ciphertext) and Decaps (Bob opens it).

Five families of hard problems

LatticesFinding the nearest point in a huge multi-dimensional grid (2.1 to 2.4). The main family.
Hash functionsReversing a fingerprint (3.1). The most conservative choice.
Error-correcting codesFixing errors in a scrambled code (3.2). Studied since 1978.
Multivariate equationsSolving many quadratic equations at once (3.3).
IsogeniesFinding paths between elliptic curves (3.3). One scheme was broken in 2022.
None of these problems has a known fast quantum algorithm. Shor's trick needs a hidden period (1.1), and these problems don't have one.

The new standards

In 2016 the US standards agency NIST invited the world to submit and attack candidates. After 82 submissions and years of public analysis:

ML-DSA (FIPS 204, 2024)CRYSTALS-DilithiumSignatures (main choice)Lattice

Security levels

Each scheme comes in sizes matched to how hard AES is to break: Level 1 ≈ AES-128, Level 3 ≈ AES-192, Level 5 ≈ AES-256. Bigger level, bigger keys, more safety margin.

Part 2 · Lattice Cryptography

Hiding secrets in giant grids: the maths behind the main new standards.

2.1 Lattices: the grid that hides secrets

A lattice is a regular grid of points, like the holes in a pegboard or the atoms in a crystal. It is the foundation of ML-KEM, ML-DSA and FN-DSA.

Building a lattice from a basis

Pick two arrows, the basis vectors. Every lattice point is a whole-number combination of them: walk some whole number of steps along the first arrow and some along the second.

Example. Basis g₁ = (2, 0) and g₂ = (1, 2).
1·g₁ + 1·g₂ = (3, 2) is a lattice point. 2·g₁ − 1·g₂ = (3, −2) is too. But (2.5, 1) is not: it would need half-steps.

Same grid, different bases

The same lattice can be built from very different bases. b₁ = (7, 2) and b₂ = (5, 2) produce exactly the same points as g₁ and g₂: for example (7, 2) = 3g₁ + g₂, and (2, 0) = b₁ − b₂.

A good basisShort arrows, nearly at right angles. Easy to work with.
A bad basisLong arrows, nearly pointing the same way. Hides the grid's structure.

This is the trick of lattice cryptography: the private key is a good basis, and the public key is a bad basis of the same lattice.

The hard problems

Shortest Vector Problem (SVP)Find the lattice point closest to the origin (other than the origin itself).
Closest Vector Problem (CVP)Given any point in space, find the lattice point nearest to it.

In 2 dimensions you can solve these by eye. Cryptography uses lattices with hundreds of dimensions, where the number of nearby points to check grows exponentially. The best known algorithms, quantum included, would take longer than the age of the universe.

Worked example: CVP with each basis. Target t = (4.4, 1.3). The simple method: write t in terms of the basis, then round to whole numbers.
Good basis: t = 1.875·g₁ + 0.65·g₂ → round to 2·g₁ + 1·g₂ = (5, 2), distance 0.92. This is the true closest point. ✓
Bad basis: t = 0.575·b₁ + 0.075·b₂ → round to 1·b₁ + 0·b₂ = (7, 2), distance 2.69. Wrong! ✗

With the good basis, rounding works. With the bad basis it fails, and in hundreds of dimensions nobody knows how to fix it.

2.2 Learning with errors (LWE): equations with a little noise

ML-KEM and ML-DSA are built on a puzzle called Learning With Errors (Oded Regev, 2005). It turns the lattice problem into simple sums with a small amount of deliberate noise.

Easy without noise

Suppose there is a secret pair s = (s₁, s₂), and you are given sums on a 17-clock (mod 17):

4·s₁ + 1·s₂ = 0 (mod 17)
7·s₁ + 3·s₂ = 2 (mod 17)

Two equations, two unknowns: school algebra (Gaussian elimination) solves them at once. The answer is s = (3, 5). Check: 4·3 + 5 = 17 → 0 ✓, and 7·3 + 3·5 = 36 → 2 ✓.

Hard with a little noise

Now add a tiny random error e (−1, 0 or +1) to each answer before publishing it:

Rowaa·s mod 17error ePublished b
1(4, 1)0+11
2(7, 3)2−11
3(2, 8)12012
4(5, 6)11+112

The public sees only the a's and b's. Now elimination breaks down: every step mixes and grows the hidden errors until the answer is garbage. With hundreds of unknowns and well-chosen noise, finding s is as hard as solving a lattice problem. That is the LWE problem.

Why lattices? The rows a, together with the clock, define a lattice; the true values a·s are a lattice point, and the noisy b's are a point near it. Removing the noise means solving CVP (2.1).

Encrypting one bit with LWE

Here is a complete toy version of Regev's encryption, using the table above. Private key: s = (3, 5). Public key: the four rows (a, b).

Encrypt. Alice secretly picks some rows, say rows 1, 3 and 4, and adds them up:

u = (4+2+5, 1+8+6) = (11, 15) (mod 17), and v = 1 + 12 + 12 = 25 → 8 (mod 17).

To send bit 0 she sends (u, v = 8). To send bit 1 she adds "half the clock" (8) to v: (u, v = 16).

Decrypt. Bob computes u·s = 11·3 + 15·5 = 108 → 6 (mod 17), then v − 6:

Bit 0: 8 − 6 = 2, close to 0, so the bit is 0. ✓
Bit 1: 16 − 6 = 10, close to 8 (half the clock), so the bit is 1. ✓

The leftover 2 is exactly the sum of the chosen errors: +1 + 0 + 1. It is small, so it doesn't spoil the answer.

Picture the 17-clock: results in the top half (near 0) decode as 0, results in the bottom half (near 8) as 1. Our two results, 2 and 10, are marked. The noise nudges the result a little but, as long as the total noise stays small, never across the border.

2.3 ML-KEM (Kyber): the new key exchange

ML-KEM (formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber) is the main post-quantum key exchange, standardized as FIPS 203 in 2024 and already used by major browsers. It is LWE (2.2) made fast and compact.

Numbers become polynomials

Plain LWE needs huge tables of numbers. ML-KEM packs 256 numbers into one polynomial: an expression like 3 + 5x + 2x² + … + 7x²⁵⁵, where each coefficient is a number on a clock of size q = 3329.

Polynomials are multiplied as usual, with one wrap-around rule: x²⁵⁶ = −1. So the result never grows past x²⁵⁵. Here is the same rule on a small scale, with x⁴ = −1:

(x³ + 2) · (x + 1) = x⁴ + x³ + 2x + 2. Replace x⁴ by −1: −1 + x³ + 2x + 2 = x³ + 2x + 1.

One polynomial multiplication does the work of a whole 256 × 256 table, and a clever shortcut (the number-theoretic transform, a fast way to multiply polynomials using clock arithmetic) makes it very fast.

Small blocks called modules

Instead of one giant polynomial, ML-KEM uses a small k × k grid of them, called a module. Changing k changes the security level:

VersionkLevelPublic keyCiphertextShared secret
ML-KEM-51221800 bytes768 bytes32 bytes
ML-KEM-768331,184 bytes1,088 bytes32 bytes
ML-KEM-1024451,568 bytes1,568 bytes32 bytes

How a key exchange runs

It follows the toy encryption from 2.2 exactly, with polynomials in place of numbers:

  1. KeyGen (Bob): picks a small secret s and small noise e, publishes the public key (A, t = A·s + e). A is a grid of random polynomials, made from a short seed so it doesn't need to be sent in full.
  2. Encaps (Alice): picks a random 32-byte message m, uses fresh small r, e₁, e₂ and computes u = Aᵀ·r + e₁ and v = tᵀ·r + e₂ + m·(q/2), just like adding "half the clock". She sends (u, v) and derives the shared key K from m with a hash.
  3. Decaps (Bob): computes v − sᵀ·u, which is m·(q/2) plus small noise, rounds each coefficient to 0 or q/2 to recover m, and derives the same K.
A safety check (the Fujisaki–Okamoto transform). After decrypting, Bob re-encrypts m himself and checks that he gets exactly the same ciphertext. If a forged ciphertext is detected, he returns a useless random key. This blocks attackers who send trick ciphertexts to learn about s.

Speed and size

ML-KEM is faster than RSA and elliptic curves: generating keys and exchanging a secret takes a fraction of a millisecond. Its only cost is size: about 1 KB each way, against 32 bytes for elliptic curves. Bigger, but still tiny next to a typical web page.

2.4 Lattice signatures: ML-DSA and FN-DSA

Signatures use the same lattice ideas. The challenge: show you know the secret without the signature leaking it.

Signatures in three steps

1. CommitThe signer picks a random "mask" y and publishes a fingerprint w of it.
2. ChallengeA hash of the message and w produces a small challenge c, which nobody can predict or choose.
3. RespondThe signer sends z = y + c·s. The mask y hides the secret s.

The verifier checks the answer using only public values. This recipe is called Fiat–Shamir, after the people who showed how a hash can replace a live questioner.

Toy example with numbers

Clock q = 97, public number a = 10. Private key s = 3. Public key t = a·s = 30.

Commit: random mask y = −5, so w = a·y = −50 → 47 (mod 97).

Challenge: the hash of (message, 47) gives c = 2.

Respond: z = y + c·s = −5 + 2·3 = 1. The signature is (c = 2, z = 1).

Verify: a·z − c·t = 10·1 − 2·30 = −50 → 47 = w ✓. The verifier rebuilds w, re-hashes, and gets the same c.

(In this toy, s could be found by dividing 30 by 10. In real ML-DSA, s is a short vector of polynomials and t = A·s + e is an LWE sample, so it can't be undone.)

Rejection, or "with aborts"

If z = y + c·s comes out unusually large, it could hint at s (a big z suggests a big s pushed it there). So the signer throws it away and tries again with a new mask. Only signatures inside a safe range are published.

With s = 3 and c = 2, only z between −4 and 4 is published. Mask y = −5 gives z = 1: accept. Mask y = 8 gives z = 14: too big, reject and retry. On average ML-DSA needs a handful of tries per signature, which is still very fast.

This idea is called Fiat–Shamir with aborts (Vadim Lyubashevsky), and it is the heart of ML-DSA.

Its signatures are much smaller, but it needs careful floating-point maths (the fast Fourier transform) to avoid leaking secrets.

3.1 Hash-based signatures: security from fingerprints alone

If you trust nothing else, you can still trust a good hash function. Hash-based signatures rely only on that, which makes them the most conservative choice.

Hash functions

A hash function turns any input into a short fixed-size fingerprint. Real ones such as SHA-256 have three properties:

Easy forwardComputing H(x) is fast.
One-wayGiven a fingerprint, you can't find an input that produces it.
Collision-resistantYou can't find two inputs with the same fingerprint.

For the numbers below we use a toy hash, H(x) = (x² + 7) mod 101. It is far too small to be secure, but it shows the mechanics.

Lamport one-time signature

Leslie Lamport's 1979 idea: for each message bit, prepare two secret numbers (one for "0", one for "1") and publish their hashes.

Private key (2-bit messages): bit 1: (17, 42), bit 2: (63, 88).

Public key = hashes: H(17) = 94, H(42) = 54, H(63) = 37, H(88) = 75 → (94, 54), (37, 75).

Sign the message 10: bit 1 is 1, so reveal 42; bit 2 is 0, so reveal 63. Signature = (42, 63).

Verify: H(42) = 1764 + 7 = 1771 → 54 ✓ (bit 1's "1" slot). H(63) = 3976 → 37 ✓ (bit 2's "0" slot).

Forging "11" would need a number that hashes to 75. That means reversing the hash, which is impossible for a real hash.

Only once! After signing 10 and then 01, all four secrets are public and anyone can sign anything. That is why it is a one-time signature.

Shorter signatures with hash chains

Lamport signatures are large. Winternitz signatures sign several bits at once using a chain: hash the secret again and again.

Secret 5. Chain: 5 → H → 32 → H → 21 → H → 44. Public key = 44 (the end of the chain).

Sign the digit 1: reveal the value after 1 hash, 32.

Verify: hash 32 two more times: 32 → 21 → 44 ✓ (1 + 2 = 3 steps in total).

Anyone can walk forward along the chain, so a checksum digit is added to stop forgers from turning a 1 into a 2.

Merkle trees

One-time keys are awkward: you would need a new public key for every message. Ralph Merkle's fix: make many one-time key pairs, hash their public keys together in pairs, and keep hashing up to a single root. Only the root is published.

Pair-hash rule (toy): H₂(x, y) = (3x + y) mod 101.

H₂(94, 37) = 319 → 16; H₂(12, 60) = 96 → 96; root = H₂(16, 96) = 144 → 43.

Signing with leaf 2 (37): send the one-time signature, the leaf 37, and its authentication path: the neighbours 94 and 96 (dashed orange).

Verify: H₂(94, 37) = 16, then H₂(16, 96) = 43 = root ✓.

A tree of height h holds 2ʰ one-time keys, and each signature needs only h neighbours. XMSS and LMS are standardized versions; they are stateful: the signer must remember which leaves are used, because reusing one is fatal.

SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+): no memory needed

SLH-DSA removes the state by using a huge tree of trees (a "hypertree") and picking the leaf from a hash of the message, so the same leaf is practically never chosen twice. It also uses a few-time signature (FORS) at the bottom.

StrengthsSecurity rests only on hash functions, the best-understood tools in cryptography. Public key just 32 bytes.
CostsSignatures of 7,856 bytes (small, slow version) to 17,088 bytes (fast version) at level 1, and signing is slower than ML-DSA.

3.2 Code-based cryptography: hiding messages in errors

Phones, Wi-Fi and spacecraft all use error-correcting codes to repair bits damaged in transit. Robert McEliece realized in 1978 that this repair skill can be a secret key.

The Hamming(7,4) code

Richard Hamming's code turns 4 data bits into 7 by adding 3 parity checks (a parity bit says whether a group of bits holds an even or odd number of 1s), so that any single flipped bit can be found and fixed. Positions 1, 2 and 4 hold checks; positions 3, 5, 6 and 7 hold data.

Encode data 1011 (d₁ = 1, d₂ = 0, d₃ = 1, d₄ = 1):

p₁ = d₁ ⊕ d₂ ⊕ d₄ = 0, p₂ = d₁ ⊕ d₃ ⊕ d₄ = 1, p₃ = d₂ ⊕ d₃ ⊕ d₄ = 0.

Codeword (positions 1–7: p₁ p₂ d₁ p₃ d₂ d₃ d₄) = 0110011.

Noise flips position 6: received 0110001.

Decode: check positions {1,3,5,7}: 0⊕1⊕0⊕1 = 0. Check {2,3,6,7}: 1⊕1⊕0⊕1 = 1. Check {4,5,6,7}: 0⊕0⊕0⊕1 = 1. Read the checks bottom-up as binary: 110 = 6. Flip position 6 back. ✓

McEliece, errors as a lock

For small codes, anyone can decode. But for a huge, randomly scrambled code, decoding is extremely hard unless you know the hidden structure:

  1. Private key: a large code with a fast decoder (a "Goppa code" that can fix t errors), plus secret scrambling.
  2. Public key: the scrambled code, which looks completely random.
  3. Encrypt: turn the message into a codeword of the public code, then deliberately flip t random bits.
  4. Decrypt: unscramble and use the fast decoder to remove the errors. Without the secret structure, Eve faces general decoding, a problem that has stayed hard for over 45 years.
Classic McElieceVery well studied. Tiny ciphertexts (about 100 bytes) but enormous public keys: about 261 KB at level 1, over 1 MB at level 5.
HQCChosen by NIST in 2025 as a backup to ML-KEM, so that a surprise attack on lattices wouldn't leave the world without a standard. Keys and ciphertexts of a few KB.

3.3 Multivariate and isogeny cryptography

Two more families round out the picture: one built on systems of equations, one on elliptic curves. Both have taught important lessons.

Multivariate equations

Solving one equation with one unknown is easy. Solving many quadratic equations (with terms like x₁·x₂) in many unknowns at once is very hard, even with bits only.

Toy public key (bits, mod 2): P₁ = x₁x₂ + x₃ and P₂ = x₂x₃ + x₁.

Sign the message (1, 1): find bits x with P₁ = 1 and P₂ = 1. The signer, who knows a hidden shortcut, gives x = (1, 1, 0).

Verify: P₁ = 1·1 + 0 = 1 ✓, P₂ = 1·0 + 1 = 1 ✓. Checking is just plugging in.

With 3 variables you could try all 8 options. Real schemes use around 100 variables: 2¹⁰⁰ options.

The signer's shortcut is a secret, easy-to-solve system disguised by scrambling the variables. Schemes such as UOV ("unbalanced oil and vinegar") have short signatures but large public keys. Rainbow, a 2020 NIST finalist, was broken in 2022 by a weekend's computing on a laptop, a reminder that new schemes need years of testing.

Isogenies

An isogeny is a special map from one elliptic curve (1.2) to another. Picture a vast network of curves connected by such maps: walking a random path is easy, but finding a path between two given curves seems very hard, even for quantum computers.

Isogeny schemes have the smallest keys of all. But the most famous one, SIKE, was broken in 2022 with a classical attack that took about an hour on a single computer, because its public keys revealed extra information. Newer designs such as SQIsign (very compact signatures) avoid that weakness and are still being studied.

Lesson: a scheme isn't trusted just because quantum computers can't break it. It must also survive years of classical attack. That is why NIST picked lattices plus backups from different families.

4.2 Migrating to PQC: how the world switches

Swapping the cryptography of the whole internet is a huge job. Here is how it is being done.

Hybrid key exchange

PQC schemes are young. To be safe during the transition, systems run a classical and a post-quantum key exchange together and combine the results:

Why it is safe. An attacker must break both. With a quantum computer they can recover K₁, but K₂ is still unknown, and hashing K₁ together with an unknown K₂ gives a key they know nothing about. And if ML-KEM ever had a flaw, X25519 still protects today's traffic.

This hybrid, called X25519MLKEM768, has been switched on by default in major browsers and large websites since 2024. Messaging apps such as Signal and iMessage added post-quantum protection in 2023 and 2024.

Steps for an organization

  1. Inventory: find every place that uses RSA, Diffie–Hellman or ECC: websites, VPNs, code signing, smart cards, databases.
  2. Prioritize: data that must stay secret for years (Mosca's rule, 1.2) and systems that are hard to update come first.
  3. Crypto-agility: design systems so the algorithm can be swapped by changing a setting, not rewriting software.
  4. Test and deploy: start with hybrids, watch for problems with larger messages, then move fully to PQC.

Part 5 · Quantum Cryptography Basics

The quantum ideas behind physics-based security, and why copying a qubit is impossible.

5.1 Quantum toolkit: qubits, bases and entanglement

Quantum cryptography uses the laws of physics instead of hard maths. This section gives the handful of quantum ideas it needs, building on the spinning-coin picture of a qubit from 1.1.

Kets and amplitudes

Physicists write quantum states inside a ket, a bar and an angle bracket: |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the qubit's two basic states.

A qubit in superposition is written a|0⟩ + b|1⟩. The numbers a and b are amplitudes. When you look, you get 0 with chance a² and 1 with chance b², and the chances add to 1.

Example. |+⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩. Chance of 0 = 0.71² ≈ 0.5, chance of 1 ≈ 0.5: a fair coin.

|−⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ − 0.71|1⟩ gives the same 50/50 odds. The minus sign (the phase) does not change the odds here, but it makes |−⟩ a genuinely different state from |+⟩.

States as arrows on the Bloch circle

Each state can be drawn as an arrow: |0⟩ points up, |1⟩ down, |+⟩ right and |−⟩ left. Opposite arrows are states that can be told apart perfectly. In full 3D this circle becomes the Bloch sphere, whose third axis holds two more states, |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ (pointing toward and away from you).

Angles give odds. If two arrows are an angle θ apart, the chance one state "looks like" the other is cos²(θ/2). |0⟩ and |+⟩ are 90° apart: cos²(45°) = 0.5. |0⟩ and |1⟩ are 180° apart: cos²(90°) = 0.

Bases and measurement

A basis is a pair of opposite arrows you choose to measure along. The two used most are:

Z basis (+ shape)|0⟩ and |1⟩: up and down
X basis (× shape)|+⟩ and |−⟩: right and left

Measuring forces the qubit to pick one arrow of the basis, with the odds above, and the qubit then collapses: it becomes the arrow it picked and forgets its old state.

StateMeasured in ZMeasured in X
|0⟩always 0+ or − (50/50)
|+⟩0 or 1 (50/50)always +
Measuring in the wrong basis gives a random answer and destroys the original state. This one fact is the heart of quantum key distribution.

Two qubits and gates

Two qubits have four basic states: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. To combine two separate qubits, multiply them out like brackets in algebra (this is called the tensor product):

|+⟩|0⟩ = (0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩)|0⟩ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|10⟩

A gate is an operation that changes qubits. Gates are linear: they act on each part of a superposition separately and keep the amplitudes. The CNOT gate flips the second qubit when the first is 1, so the second becomes a ⊕ b:

In|00⟩|01⟩|10⟩|11⟩
Out|00⟩|01⟩|11⟩|10⟩

Entanglement and Bell states

Some two-qubit states can't be split into "first qubit times second qubit". They are entangled. The most famous is the Bell state:

Φ⁺ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩

Each qubit alone is a fair coin (50/50). But measured in the same basis, the two results always match: 00 or 11, never 01 or 10, even if the qubits are far apart.

There are four Bell states in all. Entangled pairs power the E91 protocol (6.2) and the Bell test (6.7).

5.2 Classical cryptographic models: how secrets are kept today

Cryptography is the science of keeping messages secret.

Alice, Bob and Eve

Every story in cryptography has the same three characters:

Alice wants to send a secret to Bob. Eve can see (and maybe change) everything that travels between them. The message before locking is the plaintext, the locked message is the ciphertext, and the secret number used to lock and unlock it is the key.

The method is public

In 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote down a rule still used today: assume Eve knows exactly how your lock works. Only the key is secret. Good locks are published and studied by everyone; if security depends on hiding the method, it breaks as soon as the method leaks.

Passive EveOnly listens and copies what passes by.
Active EveCan also block, change or fake messages, or pretend to be Bob ("man in the middle").

Symmetric (shared-key) cryptography

Alice and Bob use the same key to lock and unlock, like a house key both of them carry. A simple lock is XOR (⊕, 1.1): add the key bit by bit.

Example. Message 1011, key 0110.
Lock: 1011 ⊕ 0110 = 1101 (this is what Eve sees).
Unlock: 1101 ⊕ 0110 = 1011. XOR-ing the same key twice cancels out.

Real systems such as AES mix the bits far more thoroughly, but the idea is the same. Symmetric locks are fast, and Grover's algorithm only weakens them a little (1.1).

The catch: the key distribution problem. Before they can talk secretly, Alice and Bob must already share a key. How do they agree on one if Eve hears everything they say? This is the problem quantum cryptography solves.

Public-key cryptography

Here each person has two keys: a public key that locks (anyone may have it) and a private key that unlocks (kept secret). It is like a padlock you hand out open: anyone can snap it shut, only you can open it. RSA (1.1) works this way.

Toy RSA with tiny numbers. Bob picks primes 3 and 11, so N = 33. His public key is (N = 33, e = 3); his private key is d = 7.
Alice locks the message m = 4: 4³ = 64, and 64 mod 33 = 31. She sends 31.
Bob unlocks: 31⁷ mod 33 = 4. ✓ (31 is −2 on a 33-clock, and (−2)⁷ = −128, which wraps to 4.)
Eve sees N = 33 and 31. To find d she must factor 33 = 3 × 11, which is easy here but impossibly hard for a 600-digit N on an ordinary computer.

Two kinds of security

Computational security"Breaking it would take too long." Safe only while nobody has a faster method or a faster computer. RSA is in this group, and Shor's algorithm is exactly such a faster method.
Information-theoretic security"Breaking it is impossible, even with unlimited computing power, because the information simply isn't there." This is the gold standard, and the next section shows how it works.
Two answers to the quantum threat: post-quantum cryptography uses new kinds of maths that quantum computers can't easily break (still computational security). Quantum cryptography, the subject of this part, uses the laws of physics to reach information-theoretic security.

5.3 Information-theoretic security: unbreakable in principle

Is there any lock that no amount of computing power can ever open? Yes, and it is surprisingly simple.

The one-time pad

Use the XOR lock from 5.2 with a key that follows three strict rules:

1. Truly randomEvery key bit is a fair coin toss.
2. As long as the messageOne key bit for every message bit.
3. Used only onceThrown away after one message (hence "one-time").

In 1949 Claude Shannon proved that this one-time pad has perfect secrecy: the ciphertext tells Eve nothing at all about the message.

Why: every message is equally possible

Example. Eve sees the ciphertext 1101. Could the message be 1011? Yes, if the key was 0110. Could it be 0000? Yes, if the key was 1101. Could it be 1111? Yes, with key 0010. For every 4-bit message there is exactly one key that fits, and every key was equally likely. So all 16 messages are still equally likely: Eve has learned nothing.

Try the one-time pad

Tap the message bits to change them. Then play Eve: tap a guess, and see the key that would make your guess "correct".

Message (tap to change)
Key (random)

Reuse and key supply

Never reuse the key. If two messages share a key, XOR-ing the two ciphertexts makes the key cancel out:

m₁ = 1010, m₂ = 0110, same key k = 1100.
c₁ = 1010 ⊕ 1100 = 0110 and c₂ = 0110 ⊕ 1100 = 1010.
Eve computes c₁ ⊕ c₂ = 0110 ⊕ 1010 = 1100 = m₁ ⊕ m₂. The key has vanished, and patterns in the messages start to leak.

So the one-time pad needs a huge supply of fresh, shared, random key. Physically carrying keys around is slow and risky. Quantum key distribution (6.1) is a way to grow such keys over a cable or through the air.

Measuring information in bits

Later sections need one more idea: how much Eve knows, counted in bits. Shannon's entropy measures how unpredictable something is:

Fair coin (50/50)Completely unpredictable: 1 bit of uncertainty.
Two-headed coinAlways heads: 0 bits. Nothing to learn.
Biased coin (97/3)Almost predictable: only about 0.19 bits.

For a yes/no event with chance p the formula is h(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p). Drag the slider to see it:

Why this matters: if Bob's copy of the key differs from Alice's in a fraction Q of the bits, it takes about h(Q) bits of extra talk per key bit to fix the errors (6.5). And Eve's knowledge, measured in these bits, is what must be squeezed out (6.6).

5.4 The no-cloning theorem: quantum states can't be copied

Copying is easy for ordinary data: you can photocopy a page or duplicate a file perfectly. In 1982 Wootters, Zurek and Dieks proved that no machine can make a perfect copy of an unknown qubit. This one fact is the foundation of quantum cryptography.

A copier that seems to work

Let's try to build a copier. It takes the qubit to copy and a blank qubit in |0⟩, and should output two copies. The CNOT gate (5.1) looks perfect:

Copy |0⟩CNOT |0⟩|0⟩ = |0⟩|0⟩ ✓ two copies of |0⟩
Copy |1⟩CNOT |1⟩|0⟩ = |1⟩|1⟩ ✓ two copies of |1⟩

It copies 0 and 1 perfectly. So far so good.

Where it fails: superpositions

Now feed it |+⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩. Gates act on each part of a superposition separately (they are linear, 5.1), so:

What the copier gives: 0.71|0⟩|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩|1⟩ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩. That's the Bell state Φ⁺ (5.1), an entangled pair!

What two copies would be: |+⟩|+⟩ = (0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩)(0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩) = 0.5|00⟩ + 0.5|01⟩ + 0.5|10⟩ + 0.5|11⟩ (tensor product, 5.1).

These are different: the copier has no |01⟩ or |10⟩ at all. The copy failed.

This isn't just a bad choice of copier. Any machine that copies |0⟩ and |1⟩ correctly must, by linearity, turn |+⟩ into 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩, never into |+⟩|+⟩. And a machine tuned to copy |+⟩ and |−⟩ fails on |0⟩ and |1⟩. No machine copies them all.

Part 6 · Quantum Key Distribution

Sharing a secret key with single photons, and catching anyone who listens.

6.1 Quantum key distribution: the BB84 protocol

Quantum key distribution (QKD) lets Alice and Bob grow a shared random key, ready for a one-time pad, so that any eavesdropping is detected. The first and most famous recipe is BB84 (Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard, 1984).

Qubits made of light

QKD sends qubits as single photons, the smallest possible pieces of light. A light wave wiggles in some direction as it travels; this direction is its polarization. Polarized sunglasses work by only letting one direction through.

BB84 uses four directions, grouped into two bases (5.1):

Measuring a photon in the same basis it was sent in gives the right bit every time. Measuring in the other basis gives a random result, 50/50, and destroys the original (5.1). Just like |0⟩ measured in the +/− basis.

The four steps of BB84

  1. Send. For each photon Alice picks a random bit and a random basis, and sends the matching polarization.
  2. Measure. Bob doesn't know her bases, so he picks a random basis for each photon and measures.
  3. Sift. Over an ordinary public channel they compare bases only (never the bits). They keep the bits where the bases matched (about half) and throw away the rest. The kept bits are the sifted key.
  4. Check. They publicly compare a small random sample of the sifted key. If too many differ, someone was listening (6.3, 6.4).
The public channel may be heard by Eve, but it must be authenticated: Bob must be sure he is really talking to Alice. A small shared starter key does this, so QKD actually "grows" a long key from a short one.

6.2 QKD protocols and their types

BB84 started a whole family of protocols. They differ in what is sent and where the security comes from.

Two big families

Prepare-and-measureAlice prepares each qubit and sends it; Bob measures it. Examples: BB84, B92, six-state.
Entanglement-basedA source in the middle sends one half of an entangled pair (5.1) to each of them. Examples: E91, BBM92.

B92 with just two states

Bennett showed in 1992 that two polarizations are enough, as long as they are not opposite: Alice sends → for 0 and ↗ for 1. Bob measures in a random basis and only keeps results that are impossible for one of the two states:

Bob measures in + and gets ↑. A → photon never gives ↑ in the + basis, so Alice must have sent ↗: the bit is 1, for sure.

Bob measures in × and gets ↖. A ↗ photon never gives ↖, so she sent →: the bit is 0.

Any other result (→ or ↗) could have come from either state, so it is discarded. Chance of a keepable result: ½ (right basis to rule one out) × ½ (the outcome lands on the "impossible" side) = 25%.

B92 is simpler to build but wastes more photons than BB84 (which keeps 50%).

Six-state protocol

This protocol adds a third basis, the circular one, matching the y-axis of the Bloch sphere (|+i⟩ and |−i⟩, 5.1). Only 1 in 3 bases match, so fewer bits survive sifting. In exchange Eve is easier to catch:

Intercept-resend (6.3) with three bases: Eve picks the wrong basis 2/3 of the time, and then Bob's bit is wrong half the time: 2/3 × 1/2 = 33% errors, compared with 25% in BB84.

E91 and BBM92: using entanglement

In E91 (Artur Ekert, 1991) a source sends each of them one half of a Bell pair Φ⁺ (5.1). When they happen to measure in the same direction, their results always match, so these become key bits. Results from different directions aren't wasted: they are used for a Bell test (6.7), which proves that nobody has tampered with the pairs.

BBM92 (1992) is the entangled version of BB84: both sides measure their half in + or × at random and sift just like BB84.

A neat bonus: with entanglement, the key doesn't exist anywhere until Alice and Bob measure. Not even the source knows it.

6.3 Eavesdropping strategies: what Eve can try

Eve wants to learn the key without being noticed. Here are her main options, from simplest to most powerful, and why each one leaves traces.

Intercept and resend

Eve catches each photon, measures it in a random basis, and sends Bob a new photon matching what she saw. Follow one photon from the sifted key (where Alice and Bob used the same basis):

The numbers. Chance of an error = ½ (Eve picks the wrong basis) × ½ (Bob's measurement then lands on the wrong bit) = ¼ = 25%. In a 1,000-bit sifted key, about 250 bits would differ. Meanwhile Eve knows only the bits where she happened to pick the right basis: about half.

If Eve only attacks a fraction f of the photons, the error rate is f ÷ 4. Try it:

Photon-number splitting

Real QKD often uses a very dim laser instead of a perfect single-photon source. Now and then a pulse contains two photons with the same polarization. Eve can split one off, keep it, and let the other reach Bob untouched: no errors at all.

How often? With an average of 0.1 photons per pulse, the laws of chance (a Poisson distribution) give: 90.5% of pulses are empty, 9.05% hold one photon, and 0.47% hold two or more. So among pulses that carry anything, about 1 in 20 can be split.

The fix: decoy states. Alice randomly mixes in pulses of different brightness. Eve can't tell decoys from real pulses, so if she favours multi-photon pulses, the detection rates of the decoys come out wrong and she is caught.

Cloning and smarter attacks

Approximate cloningEve uses the best possible imperfect copier (5.4). She keeps one copy and forwards the other, but the forwarded copy is only about 83% faithful, causing errors.
Individual attacksEve probes each photon on its own and measures her probe immediately (intercept-resend is one).
Collective attacksEve attaches a quantum probe to each photon, stores all probes in a quantum memory, and measures them together after hearing the public discussion.
Coherent attacksThe most general: anything quantum physics allows, on all photons together. Modern security proofs cover even these.
The key fact behind every security proof: information gained by Eve always costs disturbance. The more she learns, the higher the error rate Alice and Bob see.

Attacks around the physics

Man in the middleEve pretends to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob. Stopped by authenticating the public channel (6.1).
Side channels and hackingAttacking the real equipment: blinding Bob's detectors with bright light, or shining light into Alice's device to read its settings (a "Trojan horse"). Stopped by careful engineering, and by MDI and device-independent QKD (6.2).

6.4 Error rate estimation: is anyone listening?

After sifting, Alice and Bob must find out how many of their bits disagree. That number is their eavesdropping alarm.

The quantum bit error rate (QBER)

The QBER is the fraction of sifted bits where Bob's bit differs from Alice's:

QBER = number of mismatched bits ÷ number of bits compared

To estimate it they publicly compare a random sample of the sifted key, then throw those bits away (Eve heard them).

Example. 1,000 sifted bits. They compare 100 bits chosen at random and find 3 that differ. Estimated QBER = 3 ÷ 100 = 3%. The remaining 900 bits are kept for the key.

Even with no Eve, real equipment gives a few percent of errors from noise in the fibre and imperfect detectors. Alice and Bob can't tell noise from Eve, so they play safe: every error is assumed to be Eve's work.

How much can a sample be trusted?

A sample is like a poll: it gives an estimate, not the exact value. The bigger the sample, the closer the estimate. A rough guide for the "wobble" is √(Q × (1 − Q) ÷ n):

With Q = 3% and n = 100 compared bits: √(0.03 × 0.97 ÷ 100) ≈ 1.7%, so the true rate is likely between about 0% and 6.4% (within two wobbles). With n = 10,000 the wobble is just 0.17%.

Try sampling yourself. Set the true error rate (normally unknown), then sample.

Continue or abort?

QBER below about 11%Keep going. Error correction (6.5) and privacy amplification (6.6) can still produce a key that is perfectly secret.
QBER above about 11%Abort. Eve may know too much, and no secret key can be extracted. Try again later or use another line.

Where 11% comes from is shown in 6.6. For comparison: a full intercept-resend attack causes 25%, far above the limit.

6.5 Information reconciliation: fixing the errors

If the QBER is acceptable, Alice's and Bob's keys are almost the same. Information reconciliation finds and fixes the few differences by talking over the public channel, without revealing the key itself.

Order of the steps: reconciliation comes before privacy amplification, because the error-fixing chat leaks a little information that must be removed afterwards.

Parity checks

The parity of a block of bits is whether it has an even (0) or odd (1) number of 1s.

Example. Block 1011 has three 1s: odd, so parity = 1. Block 1001 has two: even, parity = 0.
If Alice's and Bob's blocks have different parities, the blocks must differ in an odd number of places (most likely one).

Worked example, binary search

Alice has 10110010 and Bob has 10100010 (one error, which they don't yet know about).

1. Whole block: Alice's key has four 1s (even, parity 0). Bob's has three 1s (odd, parity 1). Different, so there is an error somewhere.

2. Split in half. First halves: Alice 1011 (parity 1), Bob 1010 (parity 0). Different, so the error is in the first half.

3. Split again. Alice 10 (parity 1), Bob 10 (parity 1). Same, so the error is in the other quarter: Alice 11, Bob 10.

4. Last split. Bit 3: both 1, same. So the error is bit 4. Bob flips it: 10110010. ✓

Total parities announced: 4 (one per step).

Each announced parity is a bit of information Eve also hears. Finding one error among 2ᵏ bits takes about k parities.

Hunt the error

Bob's 16-bit key has one hidden error. Press Compare parity to halve the search area each time.

Cascade and the cost of talking

A single parity can't spot two errors in the same block (two flips leave the parity unchanged). The Cascade protocol fixes this by shuffling the bits and repeating the search several times with different block sizes. Modern systems often use error-correcting codes called LDPC codes instead, which work the same way in spirit.

The minimum cost. Shannon showed that fixing errors at rate Q needs at least h(Q) public bits per key bit (5.3). With 900 bits at Q = 3%: h(0.03) ≈ 0.194, so at least 900 × 0.194 ≈ 175 bits leak to Eve. Real methods leak a bit more.

6.6 Privacy amplification: squeezing out Eve's knowledge

Alice and Bob now share identical keys, but Eve may know part of them: from eavesdropping (counted by the QBER) and from the parities in reconciliation. Privacy amplification turns this long, partly-known key into a shorter key that Eve knows nothing about.

The idea with two bits

Suppose the key is two bits x₁x₂ and Eve knows x₁ but not x₂. Alice and Bob replace the two bits with one: x₁ ⊕ x₂.

Eve knows x₁ =x₂ (unknown to Eve)New key x₁ ⊕ x₂

Since x₂ is a fair coin to Eve, the new bit is a fair coin too. Eve's knowledge of x₁ has become useless. The price: the key got shorter.

Hashing a whole key

For long keys, Alice and Bob agree publicly on a random hash: a mixing rule that turns many bits into fewer, where every output bit depends on many input bits. A simple kind is a table of random 0s and 1s (called a matrix): each output bit is the parity of the key bits picked out by one row.

Key K = 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 0 (8 bits) → 4-bit final key.

Row 1 = 1 1 0 1 0 0 1 0 picks bits 1, 2, 4, 7 = 1, 0, 1, 0: two 1s, even → 0

Row 2 = 1 0 1 1 0 0 0 1 picks bits 1, 3, 4, 8 = 1, 1, 1, 0: three 1s, odd → 1

Row 3 = 0 1 0 0 1 1 1 0 picks bits 2, 5, 6, 7 = 0, 0, 1, 0: one 1, odd → 1

Row 4 = 0 0 1 0 0 1 1 1 picks bits 3, 6, 7, 8 = 1, 1, 0, 0: two 1s, even → 0

Final key = 0110. If Eve knew up to about 4 of the 8 bits, she knows essentially nothing about these 4.

The rule: shrink the key by at least as many bits as Eve might know, plus a small safety margin.

How long is the final key?

For BB84, with error rate Q, Eve's possible knowledge is about h(Q) bits per key bit, and reconciliation leaks another h(Q). So the fraction of secret bits left is:

secret key fraction r = 1 − 2·h(Q)

Q = 3%: h = 0.194, so r = 1 − 0.388 = 0.61. 61% of the bits survive.
Q = 11%: h ≈ 0.50, so r ≈ 1 − 1.0 = 0. Nothing survives. That is where the 11% limit (6.4) comes from.

6.7 Bell inequalities in cryptographic security

In 1964 John Bell found a test that tells true entanglement apart from any "pre-planned" imitation. In cryptography it becomes a lie detector: it proves that nobody, not even the maker of the equipment, could know the key.

The question Bell asked

Measure a Bell pair (5.1) and the results are perfectly linked. Could the two particles simply have agreed on their answers in advance, like twins with a shared script? Such pre-planned answers are called local hidden variables. Einstein suspected something like this. Bell showed it can be tested.

If answers were pre-planned, Eve could have planned them too, or could know the plan. So ruling out pre-planned answers also rules out Eve knowing the key.

The CHSH game

The most-used form of Bell's test is the CHSH inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969). Each round:

  1. Alice measures her particle in one of two directions, a or a′, chosen at random. Bob does the same with b or b′.
  2. Each result is +1 or −1.
  3. Over many rounds they compute the correlation E for each pair of settings: the average of (Alice's result × Bob's result). E = +1 means always equal, −1 always opposite, 0 no link.

Then they combine the four correlations:

S = E(a,b) + E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) − E(a′,b′)

Pre-planned answers can't beat 2

Suppose each particle carries fixed answers A, A′ (for Alice's two settings) and B, B′ (for Bob's), each +1 or −1. For one pair:

A·B + A·B′ + A′·B − A′·B′ = A·(B + B′) + A′·(B − B′)

Either B = B′, so (B + B′) = ±2 and (B − B′) = 0, or B = −B′, so (B + B′) = 0 and (B − B′) = ±2.

Either way the total is ±2. Example: A = A′ = B = B′ = +1 gives 1 + 1 + 1 − 1 = 2.

Averaging over many pairs can't beat the largest single value, so S ≤ 2 for any pre-planned scheme. This is Bell's inequality.

Quantum pairs reach 2.83

For the Bell pair Φ⁺, measured along directions at angles on the Bloch circle (5.1), the correlation is E = cos(angle between the directions). Choose a = 0°, a′ = 90°, b = 45°, b′ = −45°:

E(a,b) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a,b′) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a′,b) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a′,b′) = cos 135° = −0.707

S = 0.707 + 0.707 + 0.707 − (−0.707) = 2.83 (= 2√2)

2.83 is bigger than 2: no pre-planned script can produce these results. This has been confirmed in many experiments, including "loophole-free" tests in 2015, and earned the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics (Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger).

Why it protects the key

Entanglement is monogamousIf Alice's and Bob's particles are maximally linked (S ≈ 2.83), there is no link left over for a third party. Eve can't be correlated with their results.
Eve pushes S downIf Eve measures the particles on their way (intercept-resend), she breaks the entanglement and S falls to about 1.41, well below 2.
Trust no deviceDevice-independent QKD checks only that S > 2. Even if the equipment came from Eve's factory, a high S proves the key is private.
