Part 5 · Quantum Gates

The basic moves of a quantum computer: what each gate does, how it behaves, and what it is used for.

5.1 What is a quantum gate?

A normal computer works by sending bits through logic gates such as NOT, AND and OR. A quantum computer works the same way, but its gates act on qubits. A quantum gate is one basic move you make on one or more qubits.

From Parts 2 to 4 you already know everything a gate is made of:

A matrixEvery gate is a matrix that turns the old state into a new one (Part 3, 3.4).
A rotationOn the Bloch sphere, a single-qubit gate simply turns the arrow around some axis (Part 2).
UnitaryGates never lose information and never change the 100% total, so every gate can be undone (Part 3, 3.6.3).
A big difference from normal gates. An ordinary AND gate takes two bits and gives one, so you can't tell what went in. Quantum gates always have as many outputs as inputs and can always be run backwards. Nothing is ever thrown away.

How to read a circuit diagram

Quantum programs are drawn as circuits. They read like a musical score: each line is a qubit, and time runs from left to right.

5.3 The X gate: bit flip

X is the quantum version of the NOT gate. It swaps 0 and 1.

If the qubit is surely 0, X makes it surely 1, and the other way round. If the qubit is a mix, X swaps the two amounts: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ becomes β|0⟩ + α|1⟩. A qubit that was 70% likely to read 0 becomes 70% likely to read 1.

Matrix

0110
This is the swap matrix from Part 3 (also called σx).

In Dirac notation

X|0⟩ = |1⟩
X|1⟩ = |0⟩
X = |0⟩⟨1| + |1⟩⟨0| (Part 4, 4.5)

On the Bloch sphere

A half-turn (180°) around the x axis. The top (|0⟩) goes to the bottom (|1⟩). Points on the x axis, |+⟩ and |−⟩, stay where they are.

Properties

  • Self-inverse: doing X twice gets you back where you started (X·X = I).
  • Changes the chances: it swaps the chance of 0 with the chance of 1.
  • Eigenstates: |+⟩ (eigenvalue +1) and |−⟩ (eigenvalue −1) are left unchanged, apart from a sign on |−⟩.
  • One of the three Pauli gates, together with Y and Z.

Where it is used

Setting up inputsMost programs start with every qubit at |0⟩. An X gate turns any of them into a 1.
Classical logicCombined with other gates it builds reversible versions of everyday logic, such as adders.
Error modelsA random unwanted X is called a bit-flip error. Error-correction codes are designed to catch and undo it.

5.4 The Z gate: phase flip

Z leaves 0 alone and puts a minus sign on the 1 part.

Z changes α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ into α|0⟩ − β|1⟩. The chances of reading 0 or 1 do not change, because a minus sign disappears when you square. What changes is the phase: the 0 and 1 waves go from in step to opposite (Part 2, 2.2). That difference shows up as soon as other gates mix the parts.

Matrix

100−1
The sign-flip matrix from Part 3 (also called σz).

In Dirac notation

Z|0⟩ = |0⟩
Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩
Z|+⟩ = |−⟩ and Z|−⟩ = |+⟩

On the Bloch sphere

A half-turn around the z axis. The poles stay put, and points on the equator swing to the opposite side: |+⟩ becomes |−⟩.

Properties

  • Self-inverse: Z·Z = I.
  • Never changes the chances of 0 or 1. It only changes phase.
  • Eigenstates: |0⟩ (+1) and |1⟩ (−1).
  • Related to X: H·Z·H = X. Z is "X seen from a different angle".

Where it is used

Marking answersSearch algorithms such as Grover's "mark" the right answer by flipping its sign with a Z-type gate.
Error modelsAn unwanted Z is a phase-flip error, which has no counterpart in normal computers. Quantum error correction must catch these too.
Building other gatesZ, together with H, makes X. With control qubits added, it becomes CZ (5.10).

5.5 The Y gate: bit and phase flip

Y does both jobs at once: it swaps 0 and 1 and adjusts the phase.

Y swaps the 0 and 1 parts like X, and also changes their phases like Z. In fact Y = i·X·Z. On its own it is used less often than X and Z, but it completes the set of three Pauli gates, one for each axis of the Bloch sphere.

Matrix

0−ii0
(also called σy)

In Dirac notation

Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩
Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩
(the i in front is a phase you can't measure)

On the Bloch sphere

A half-turn around the y axis. Like X it takes the top to the bottom, but by a different route: X swings the arrow through the front, while Y swings it through the side.

Properties

  • Self-inverse: Y·Y = I.
  • Changes the chances: it swaps them, just like X.
  • Eigenstates: |+i⟩ (+1) and |−i⟩ (−1).
  • Relations: Y = i·X·Z. Any two different Pauli gates "anti-commute": X·Z = −Z·X.

Where it is used

Error modelsA Y error is a bit flip and phase flip together. With X and Z it covers every kind of single-qubit error.
Measuring along yAsking the y question (Part 2, 2.6) is the same as measuring Y.
Testing hardwarePauli gates are used to calibrate and benchmark real quantum chips.

5.6 The H gate: Hadamard

H is the "superposition maker". It turns a definite 0 or 1 into a perfect 50/50 mix.

H turns |0⟩ into |+⟩ (50/50, parts in step) and |1⟩ into |−⟩ (50/50, parts opposite). It is the "mixer A" from Part 3. Run it twice and you are back where you started. That is interference at work: the two paths to the wrong answer cancel (Part 3, 3.7).

Matrix

(1/√2) 111−1
the mixer A from Part 3

In Dirac notation

H|0⟩ = |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
H|1⟩ = |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2
H|+⟩ = |0⟩, H|−⟩ = |1⟩

On the Bloch sphere

A half-turn around the diagonal axis halfway between x and z. This swaps the z axis with the x axis: the top (|0⟩) goes to |+⟩ on the equator, and |+⟩ goes back to the top.

Properties

  • Self-inverse: H·H = I.
  • Swaps the questions: it exchanges the z axis and the x axis, so H·X·H = Z and H·Z·H = X.
  • Makes even mixes: from |0⟩ or |1⟩ it always gives a 50/50 chance.
  • Applied to n qubits it creates an equal mix of all 2ⁿ possible inputs at once.

Where it is used

Starting algorithmsAlmost every algorithm (Deutsch–Jozsa, Grover, Simon, Shor) starts by putting its qubits through H to explore all inputs together.
Making entanglementH followed by CNOT creates a Bell pair (5.9).
Quantum key distributionIn the BB84 protocol, the sender uses H to switch randomly between two ways of encoding bits.
Measuring along xApply H, then measure as usual, and you have asked the x question.

5.7 The S and T gates: phase turns

S, T turn the phase of the 1 part by a quarter-turn (S) or an eighth-turn (T).

Like Z, these gates leave |0⟩ alone and only change the phase of the 1 part, so they never change the chances. Z turns the phase by 180°, S by 90° and T by 45°. Think of them as finer and finer steps on the same dial: two T's make an S, and two S's make a Z.

Matrix

S = 100i   T = 100eiπ/4

In Dirac notation

S|1⟩ = i|1⟩   T|1⟩ = eiπ/4|1⟩
S|+⟩ = |+i⟩
Undo them with S† and T†, which turn the other way.

On the Bloch sphere

A turn around the z axis: 90° for S and 45° for T. The poles stay put and points on the equator move around it. S takes |+⟩ to |+i⟩.

Properties

  • Not self-inverse: S·S = Z, not I. Undo them with S† ("S-dagger") and T†.
  • Never change the chances of 0 or 1.
  • Ladder: T·T = S and S·S = Z.
  • Eigenstates: |0⟩ and |1⟩, like Z.

Where it is used

Unlocking full powerH and S alone can't do everything. Adding T makes a gate set that can build any quantum program (5.12).
Cost of a programIn error-corrected computers T gates are the most expensive step, so programs are often judged by their "T-count".
Quantum Fourier transformThe QFT, a key step inside Shor's algorithm, is built from these small phase turns.
Measuring along yApply S† and then H, and an ordinary measurement asks the y question.

5.8 Rotation gates: Rx, Ry, Rz

R gates let you turn the arrow by any angle you like, not just fixed steps.

Rx(θ), Ry(θ) and Rz(θ) turn the Bloch sphere by an angle θ around the x, y or z axis. They are like dimmer switches compared with the on/off X, Y and Z. In fact X, Y and Z are just the 180° versions, and S and T are Rz at 90° and 45°, give or take a phase you can't measure.

Matrix

Ry(θ) = cos θ/2−sin θ/2sin θ/2cos θ/2
Rz(θ) = e−iθ/200eiθ/2

In Dirac notation

Ry(θ)|0⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩
This is exactly the "tilt" slider from Part 2.

On the Bloch sphere

A turn by θ around the chosen axis. Rx and Ry change the chances because they tilt the arrow up or down. Rz only changes the phase.

Properties

  • Undo: turn by −θ. R(θ)·R(−θ) = I.
  • Adding up: two turns around the same axis add, R(a)·R(b) = R(a + b).
  • Complete: any single-qubit gate at all can be built as Rz·Ry·Rz with suitable angles.

Where it is used

Chemistry (VQE)Variational algorithms tune rotation angles, like knobs, to find a molecule's lowest energy.
Optimisation (QAOA)Uses tuned rotations to search for good solutions to scheduling and routing problems.
Quantum machine learningThe angles play the role of the "weights" that are learned during training.
Hardware controlReal chips do their rotations with timed pulses of microwaves or lasers, and the pulse length sets the angle.

Multi-qubit gates

One-qubit gates can never link qubits together. For that you need gates that act on two or more qubits at once, where one qubit's value decides what happens to another.

5.9 The CNOT gate: controlled NOT

CNOT acts on two qubits: a control and a target. If the control is 1, it flips the target. If the control is 0, it does nothing.

It is an "if" statement in a single gate: if the control is 1, apply X to the target. For ordinary 0/1 inputs the target becomes (target XOR control). This is how a quantum computer adds bits without losing information.

Something special happens when the control is a mix. Step through this two-gate circuit:

The result, (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, is the Bell state from Part 3 (3.9). The two qubits are now entangled: each one alone is a coin toss, but they always agree.

Properties

  • Self-inverse: CNOT·CNOT = I.
  • Creates entanglement when the control is in a mix. This is something no one-qubit gate can do.
  • Leaves the control unchanged for ordinary 0/1 inputs.
  • With one-qubit gates it can build any multi-qubit gate (5.12).

Where it is used

EntanglementBell pairs for quantum networks and experiments.
TeleportationSending a qubit's state using one Bell pair plus two ordinary bits.
Error correctionCNOTs copy "parity" information onto helper qubits, which reveals errors without disturbing the data.
ArithmeticIts XOR behaviour is the basic step in quantum adders.

5.10 CZ and SWAP

CZ: controlled-Z

If the control is 1, apply Z to the target. In practice this just puts a minus sign on |11⟩ and leaves the other three alone.

100001000010000−1

  • Symmetric: it doesn't matter which qubit you call the control.
  • Self-inverse.
  • CNOT = (H on target) · CZ · (H on target).

Used for: it is the natural two-qubit gate on many superconducting chips, and it builds the "cluster states" used in measurement-based quantum computing.

SWAP

Swaps the states of two qubits: |01⟩ becomes |10⟩ and the other way round.

1000001001000001

  • Self-inverse.
  • Can be built from three CNOTs in a row.
  • Doesn't create entanglement.

Used for: on real chips, each qubit can only talk to its neighbours. SWAPs move qubit states around so that the right qubits end up next to each other. This is called "routing".

5.11 The Toffoli gate (CCNOT)

CCNOT has two controls and one target. It flips the target only if both controls are 1.

If the target starts at 0, it ends up holding control 1 AND control 2. That makes Toffoli a reversible AND gate, and with it a quantum computer can do any calculation a normal computer can.

Properties

  • Self-inverse.
  • Classically complete: it can do any ordinary logic without losing information.
  • Built on real hardware from about 6 CNOTs plus several T gates.

Where it is used

ArithmeticAdders and multipliers, such as the ones Shor's algorithm uses to factor numbers.
Search oraclesChecking whether a candidate answer is correct inside Grover's search.
Error correctionSome codes use it to fix errors automatically.

5.12 Universal gate sets: how few gates do you need?

There are endless possible gates, but you don't need all of them. Just as every word can be written with 26 letters, every quantum program can be built, as accurately as you like, from a small universal set:

H, S, T and CNOT

Clifford gatesH, S and CNOT (and the Paulis). Useful, but on their own a normal computer can simulate them efficiently, so they give no speed-up.
The extra ingredientAdding T (or Toffoli) breaks that limit and unlocks full quantum power.
Hardware setsEach chip has a few "native" gates, such as rotations plus CZ, and compiles everything else from them.
Measurement isn't a gate. The meter symbol at the end of a circuit means "look at the qubit". Unlike gates, it can't be undone and it ends the mix (Part 3, 3.3).
