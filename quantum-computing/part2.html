Part 2 · The Qubit

The building block of quantum computers, and the Bloch sphere that maps it.

2.1 What is a qubit?

A classical bit is a switch: it is 0 or 1. A qubit (quantum bit) is a tiny physical object, such as a single electron, a particle of light or a small superconducting circuit, whose state can be a blend of 0 and 1.

We write this blend as |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. Read it as: "the qubit ψ is this much 0 (α) plus this much 1 (β)". Part 3 explains the notation step by step.

SuperpositionUntil you look, the qubit holds both the 0 part and the 1 part, in amounts set by α and β.
MeasurementLooking always gives a plain 0 or 1. Bigger α means a better chance of 0 (the exact chance is α squared). After you look, the blend is gone.
FragileHeat, light or vibration can scramble the blend. This is called decoherence, and it is why error correction matters.

2.2 Amplitude and phase: the qubit as a wave

Two words come up again and again in quantum computing: amplitude and phase. They are easiest to understand if you picture a qubit as a pair of small waves. This section builds the idea in five short steps.

Step 1: Why picture a qubit as a wave?

About a hundred years ago, scientists found something strange. Tiny things like electrons and particles of light don't behave only like little balls. They also behave like waves, the way ripples spread across a pond. Two ripples that meet can add up into a bigger wave or cancel each other out.

A qubit is one of these tiny things. So we picture its 0 part and its 1 part as two small waves. Any wave is described by just two things:

AmplitudeHow big the wave is (its height).
PhaseWhere the wave is in its cycle: at the top, at the bottom, or somewhere in between.

Step 2: Amplitude, "how much" of each answer

The amplitude of the 0 part says how much of the qubit is 0, and the amplitude of the 1 part says how much is 1. A bigger wave means that answer is more likely when you look.

The exact rule is simple: chance = amplitude × amplitude. An amplitude of 0.71 gives 0.71 × 0.71 ≈ 0.5, which is a 50% chance. The two chances always add up to 100%, so if one wave grows, the other must shrink.

Step 3: Phase, "where in the cycle"

A wave goes up and down over and over, like a swing. Phase says where it is in that cycle right now. An easy way to picture this is a clock hand spinning round:

  • The length of the hand is the amplitude. A longer hand makes a taller wave.
  • The direction the hand points is the phase. One full turn is one full wave, so phase is measured as an angle, from 0° to 360°.

Watch how the spinning hand draws the wave. The height of the tip, traced over time, is the wave.

This hand is what Part 3 calls a complex number: a size (amplitude) and a direction (phase) in one.

Step 4: Only the gap between phases matters

A qubit has two hands, one for the 0 part and one for the 1 part, and both keep spinning. You can never measure where a single hand points. What matters is the gap between the two hands. This gap is called the relative phase.

Think of two runners on a circular track. The race looks the same whether they are at the start line or halfway round. What matters is how far apart they are.

Turning both hands by the same amount changes nothing you can ever measure. Changing the gap does make a real difference, which you'll see in Step 5. On the Bloch sphere (2.4), the amplitudes set how far the arrow tilts down, and the relative phase sets how far it turns around.

Step 5: How phase changes amplitude

On its own, phase never changes the chances. The chance only depends on each wave's height. But quantum computers use gates, which mix the 0 part and the 1 part together. When the two waves are mixed into the same answer, they add up, and now the gap between them matters a lot:

  • In step (gap 0°): crest meets crest, and the waves add into a bigger wave. That answer becomes more likely.
  • Opposite (gap 180°): crest meets trough, and the waves cancel out. That answer becomes impossible.
  • In between: they partly cancel.

This adding and cancelling is called interference. It works just like noise-cancelling headphones, which play an opposite sound wave to cancel the noise.

Try it: change the gap and watch what happens to the combined wave (orange) and its chance.

This is the real reason quantum computers can be fast. They don't simply "try every answer at once". Programs are built so that the waves for wrong answers cancel out and the waves for the right answer add up. Part 3 (3.7) shows the maths.

2.4 What is a Bloch sphere?

The Bloch sphere is a map of every possible state of one qubit, in the same way a globe is a map of every place on Earth. It is not real space: it is a picture that turns abstract numbers into something you can see and rotate.

Why a sphere? Counting the numbers

  1. A qubit state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ uses two complex numbers, which is 4 real numbers.
  2. The chances must add up to 100% (|α|² + |β|² = 1). That removes 1, leaving 3.
  3. Shifting the phase of both parts by the same amount (turning both α and β by the same angle) changes nothing you could ever measure. That removes 1 more, leaving 2.

Two numbers are exactly what you need to name a spot on a globe: latitude and longitude. So every pure qubit state is one point on a sphere, and the arrow from the centre to that point is the state.

2.5 The three axes of the Bloch sphere

Like a globe, the Bloch sphere needs reference lines. It uses three axes at right angles to each other: x, y and z. Each axis stands for a different question you can ask the qubit, and each end of an axis is one of the two possible answers.

AxisEnds of the axisWhat it signifies
z (up–down)+z = |0⟩ (top)
−z = |1⟩ (bottom)		The answer axis. Height tells the odds of reading 0 or 1: P(0) = (1+z)/2, P(1) = (1−z)/2. Top is a sure 0, bottom a sure 1, the equator is 50/50.
x (left–right)+x = |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2
−x = |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2		Both ends are a perfect 50/50 mix. At +x the 0 part and 1 part are in step (+); at −x they are opposite (−). Asking 0-or-1 can't tell these two apart, but the x question can.
y (front–back)+y = |+i⟩ = (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2
−y = |−i⟩ = (|0⟩−i|1⟩)/√2		Also a perfect 50/50 mix, but here the two parts are a quarter-turn apart, one way (+i) or the other (−i). This is the third question, which neither z nor x can answer.
Reading a coordinate: each coordinate runs from −1 to +1 and equals (chance of the first answer) − (chance of the second). For example z = +1 means a sure 0, z = 0 means 50/50. Different books draw x pointing toward you or sideways; all that matters is that the three axes are at right angles. For those who want the formula: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩, with x = sinθ cosφ, y = sinθ sinφ, z = cosθ.

2.6 Why do we need three axes?

Each measurement answers only one question, and one answer can't pin down an arrow that can point anywhere in 3D. Each axis asks a different question, so you need all three.

Shadow analogy

Shine a light on an object and look at its shadow on the floor. The shadow is flat, and very different objects can cast the same shadow. Measuring along z is like seeing only the floor shadow: it tells you how high the arrow is (the odds of 0 or 1) but not which way around it points. Add the shadows on two walls as well, and you know the object's exact shape.

Same z answer, different states

StateAsk z: 0 or 1?Ask x: + or −?Ask y: +i or −i?
|0⟩Sure 050/5050/50

|+⟩, |−⟩ and |+i⟩ all give the same 50/50 result when you ask "0 or 1?", yet they are different states. The difference shows up later in a computation: for example, turning the sphere a quarter-turn can make |+⟩ a sure 0 and |−⟩ a sure 1. The z question alone can't see that difference, but the x and y questions can.

Why not just two axes?

The arrow's tip always sits on the sphere, so x² + y² + z² = 1. If you know x and z, you can work out how big y is, but not whether it is positive or negative: y = ±√(1 − x² − z²). For example |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ both have x = 0 and z = 0, so only the y question can tell them apart. With all three axes there is no guessing left.

Worked example: discovering an unknown qubit

Suppose someone hands you a qubit and you want to find its arrow. Ask them to make the same qubit many times (say 3000), split the copies into three groups, and ask each group a different question:

QuestionResultCoordinate = chance(first) − chance(second)
z: 0 or 1?90% got 0z = 0.9 − 0.1 = 0.8
x: + or −?80% got +x = 0.8 − 0.2 = 0.6
y: +i or −i?50% got +iy = 0.5 − 0.5 = 0

So the arrow points to (0.6, 0, 0.8). Check: 0.36 + 0 + 0.64 = 1, so it lies on the sphere. This process is called state tomography. It needs many identical copies for two reasons: measuring a qubit destroys its blend, and the laws of physics forbid making a copy of an unknown qubit (the no-cloning theorem).
