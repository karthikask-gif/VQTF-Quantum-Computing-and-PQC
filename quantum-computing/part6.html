Part 6 · The Rules of the Quantum World

Where quantum states live, the four rules they obey, and how we change and read them.

6.1 Hilbert space: where quantum states live

Every game needs a board. In quantum computing the "board" is called Hilbert space (named after the mathematician David Hilbert). It is simply the collection of every possible state a quantum system can be in.

You have already been using it. In Part 3 a qubit's state was an arrow, [α, β]. Hilbert space is the space all those arrows live in. It is like a room where each direction you can point is a different state.

What makes it a Hilbert space?

Three simple things you can do with the arrows:

  • Add them and stretch them: that is how we make mixes like α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ (superposition).
  • Measure their overlap with the inner product ⟨a|b⟩ (Part 3, 3.6.1). This gives lengths and angles, and with them chances.
  • No gaps: every in-between arrow you can imagine is also in the space.

A valid state is an arrow of length exactly 1, because its chances must add up to 100%.

6.2 Basis: the measuring sticks

To describe an arrow you need reference directions, like "east" and "north" on a map. A basis is a set of such reference directions. Every state can be written as a mix of them.

A good basis (an orthonormal basis) has two properties:

Each direction has length 1Each one is itself a valid state.
All at right anglesTheir overlaps are 0, so a measurement can tell them apart perfectly.
Just enough of themOne per dimension: 2 for a qubit, 4 for two qubits, 2ⁿ for n qubits.

The three common bases for one qubit

NameDirectionsBloch axisQuestion it asks
Computational (Z) basis|0⟩, |1⟩z (up–down)"0 or 1?"
Hadamard (X) basis|+⟩, |−⟩x"+ or −?"
Y basis|+i⟩, |−i⟩y"+i or −i?"

One state, many descriptions. Choosing a basis doesn't change the state, only how you describe it, just as one place can be given in miles or in kilometres. For example, |0⟩ written in the X basis is (|+⟩ + |−⟩)/√2. That is why measuring |0⟩ with the "+ or −?" question gives 50/50.

For two qubits the computational basis is |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. There is also a famous basis made entirely of entangled states, the Bell basis (6.7).

6.3 The postulates: four rules of the game

Chess has a few rules that every game follows. Quantum mechanics also has a short list of rules, called postulates. Nobody derives them from anything deeper: they are the starting assumptions, and every experiment so far agrees with them. Everything in this course follows from these four.

Postulate 1: StateA system's complete state is an arrow of length 1 in its Hilbert space (6.1).
Postulate 2: ChangeWhen nobody is looking, a closed system changes by a unitary operation: a reversible rotation (6.5).
Postulate 3: MeasurementMeasuring gives one result at random, with chance = |overlap|². Afterwards the state jumps to match the result (6.6).
Postulate 4: CombiningThe state space of several systems together is the tensor product of their spaces (6.4).
Notice the odd pair: rule 2 says change is smooth and reversible, while rule 3 says measurement is sudden, random and permanent. Why nature works like this is still debated. It is called the "measurement problem".

6.4 Quantum registers: groups of qubits

In a normal computer, a register is a small group of bits that hold one number together. For example, 8 bits make a byte that holds a number from 0 to 255. A quantum register is the same idea with qubits: n qubits used together as one unit.

Classical 3-bit register

Holds one of the 8 numbers 0–7 at a time. For example 101 = 5.

Quantum 3-qubit register

Can hold a mix of all 8 numbers at once, with one amplitude for each. Its state lives in an 8-dimensional Hilbert space (postulate 4).

The kets are written as binary numbers: |101⟩ means the qubits read 1, 0, 1 from left to right, which is the number 5. The leftmost qubit is the "biggest" digit.

Try it: a 3-qubit register

Flip single qubits with X, put every qubit through H to make an equal mix of all 8 numbers, then measure.

The catch: after H on all, the register holds all 8 numbers at once, but measuring gives just one of them at random. Quantum algorithms are clever because they use interference to make the useful answer the likely one before measuring.

6.5 Unitary operations: how quantum states change

Postulate 2 says that, between measurements, quantum states change only by unitary operations. Every gate in Part 5 is one. In simple terms, a unitary operation is a rotation of the state arrow.

The rules a unitary must follow

  • Keeps the length at 1: the total chance stays at exactly 100%.
  • Keeps angles: states that were perfectly distinguishable stay that way. Overlaps never change.
  • Always reversible: there is an "undo", written U† (U-dagger), with U†U = I.
  • Predictable: the same input always gives the same output state. There is no randomness here.

Examples

GatesX, H, S, CNOT… (Part 5)
Time passingEven "doing nothing" is a unitary: the laws of physics (the Schrödinger equation) slowly rotate the state.
Whole circuitsA chain of unitaries is still a unitary, so a whole circuit is one big rotation.
A surprising consequence: no copying. Because unitaries keep overlaps fixed, no unitary can make a perfect copy of an unknown qubit. This is the no-cloning theorem. It is why quantum information can't simply be backed up, and also why eavesdroppers on quantum key distribution get caught.

6.6 Quantum measurement: reading the answer

Measurement is how we get information out of a quantum computer. Postulate 3 has three parts:

1. Choose a basisYou always measure "against" a basis, for example asking "0 or 1?" (Z basis).
2. Random resultYou get one basis state, with chance |⟨result|ψ⟩|². This is called the Born rule.
3. CollapseAfterwards the qubit is that result. The old mix is gone for good.

Prepare a qubit, pick a question, and measure. Measure once to see the collapse, or measure 100 fresh copies to see the chances emerge.

Key facts about measurement

  • It can't be undone. Unlike a unitary, collapse throws information away.
  • Repeat it and you get the same answer. Measure a collapsed qubit again with the same question, and the result is certain.
  • Measuring one qubit of a register collapses only the part that matches. In an entangled pair this instantly fixes the partner's result too (6.7).
  • Other bases with a gate: hardware usually only asks "0 or 1?". To ask "+ or −?", apply H first and then measure.

6.7 Bell states: the four entangled pairs

The Bell states (named after physicist John Bell) are the four simplest and most strongly entangled states of two qubits. Each one is a perfect "linked pair": each qubit alone is a 50/50 coin toss, but the two results are always related.

NameStateAsking "0 or 1?"Asking "+ or −?"
Φ⁺ ("phi plus")(|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2Always the sameAlways the same
Φ⁻ ("phi minus")(|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2Always the sameAlways opposite
Ψ⁺ ("psi plus")(|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2Always oppositeAlways the same
Ψ⁻ ("psi minus", the singlet)(|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2Always oppositeAlways opposite

The two "types", in plain words

Φ (phi) type: "same"Asked "0 or 1?", the two qubits always agree.
Ψ (psi) type: "opposite"Asked "0 or 1?", the two qubits always disagree.
+ or − sign: the phaseInvisible to "0 or 1?", but it shows up when you ask "+ or −?". Ψ⁻ is opposite whichever way you ask.

Why they matter

TeleportationMoves a qubit's state using one Bell pair and two ordinary bits.
Superdense codingSends 2 ordinary bits by transmitting only 1 qubit of a Bell pair.
Quantum keys (E91)Shared Bell pairs give two people matching random keys, and eavesdropping can be detected.
Bell testsExperiments with these pairs proved that nature really is quantum (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2022).
Together the four Bell states form the Bell basis: four orthonormal directions for two qubits, just like |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. A "Bell measurement" asks which of the four the pair is in, and is the key step in teleportation.
