Part 1 · Quantum Introduction

The big picture first, no maths needed.

1. Classical vs. Quantum Computing

The computers we use every day are classical computers. They store everything as bits, and each bit is either 0 or 1, like a light switch that is off or on.

Quantum computers use qubits instead. A qubit follows the rules of quantum physics, which allow three new tricks:

  • Superposition: before you look, a qubit can be a mix of 0 and 1 at the same time, like a coin still spinning in the air.
  • Entanglement: two or more qubits can be linked so that their results always match up, even when they are far apart.
  • Interference: the mixes behave like waves, so they can add up or cancel out. Quantum programs are designed so wrong answers cancel and the right answer gets stronger.
AspectClassicalQuantum
Basic unitBit (0 or 1)Qubit (superposition)
OperationsLogic gates (AND, OR, NOT)Quantum gates, which gently turn the qubit's state without losing information
Key effectsSame input always gives the same outputSuperposition, entanglement, interference
OutputOne definite answerBased on chance; looking at (measuring) a qubit forces it to pick 0 or 1, and the mix is gone
ErrorsRare and easy to fixCommon; heat, light or vibration easily disturb qubits, so extra error correction is needed
MaturityDecades old and everywhereStill early; mostly in labs and the cloud
A quantum computer is not simply "a faster computer". It is only faster for certain problems, for example Shor's algorithm (splitting huge numbers into their factors very quickly) and Grover's algorithm (searching unsorted data faster).

2. Will a quantum computer replace classical computers?

No. A quantum computer is a specialist helper that works alongside a normal computer, in the same way a graphics card (GPU) helps with games and AI.

Not for everyday tasksWeb browsing, email, documents and video gain nothing from qubits.
Hard to runQubits are fragile. Many must be kept colder than outer space and shielded from any disturbance.
Narrow advantageThey help with only a few jobs: simulating molecules, factoring big numbers, and some search and planning problems.
Hybrid futureA normal computer runs the program and hands just the hardest piece to the quantum computer.

Why security still cares

Most of the internet is protected by encryption called RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC). Their safety rests on maths puzzles that normal computers would need thousands of years to solve. A large, reliable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve them quickly and break that protection.

Such a machine does not exist yet, but there is already a risk: attackers can copy encrypted data today and unlock it once the machine arrives. This is called "harvest now, decrypt later".

The answer is Post Quantum Cryptography (PQC): new kinds of encryption that run on ordinary computers but are built on puzzles quantum computers cannot solve easily either. The US standards body NIST has already published the first ones, including ML-KEM (also called Kyber, for safely agreeing on a secret key) and ML-DSA (also called Dilithium, for digital signatures).

3. Where can quantum computing be applied?

Chemistry & materialsDesigning new molecules, catalysts and better batteries.
Drug discoveryPredicting how a medicine will interact with the body's molecules.
OptimisationFinding the best delivery routes, timetables and schedules.
FinanceMeasuring risk and pricing investments.
Machine learningBeing explored, but no clear benefit has been shown yet.
CryptographyCould break today's encryption; PQC and QKD are the defences.
Engineering designDesigning antennas, optical parts and how air or water flows.
Climate & energyCapturing carbon, better solar cells, smarter power grids.
Most of these uses are still being researched. So far, quantum computers have beaten normal computers only in a few narrow lab tests.

4. Types of quantum computers

Quantum computers can be grouped in two ways: by how they compute, and by what their qubits are made of.

By how they compute

Gate-basedThe general-purpose kind. Runs programs as a series of steps (gates), like a normal processor.
Quantum annealingBuilt for one job: finding the best option. It lets the system settle into its lowest-energy state, like a ball rolling to the bottom of a valley.
Measurement-basedStarts with many linked (entangled) qubits, then computes by measuring them one by one.
TopologicalStores information in a way that resists errors by design. Still experimental.
Analog simulationCopies the behaviour of another quantum system (such as a material) to study it.

By what the qubits are made of

SuperconductingTiny electrical circuits. Fast, but must be cooled to near absolute zero (−273 °C).
Trapped ionSingle charged atoms held by electric fields. Very accurate, but slower.
PhotonicParticles of light (photons). Good for sending quantum information over networks.
Neutral atomAtoms held in place by laser beams. Easy to build in large numbers.
Spin / siliconElectrons in silicon chips. Can reuse today's chip factories.

Related technologies

Quantum communication / QKDQuantum Key Distribution: sharing secret keys so that any eavesdropping is detected by the laws of physics.
Quantum sensingUsing quantum effects to measure time, gravity or magnetic fields with extreme precision.
PQCNew encryption for normal computers that quantum computers can't break. Not itself a quantum technology.
