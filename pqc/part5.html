Part 5 · Quantum Cryptography Basics

The quantum ideas behind physics-based security, and why copying a qubit is impossible.

5.1 Quantum toolkit: qubits, bases and entanglement

Quantum cryptography uses the laws of physics instead of hard maths. This section gives the handful of quantum ideas it needs, building on the spinning-coin picture of a qubit from 1.1.

Kets and amplitudes

Physicists write quantum states inside a ket, a bar and an angle bracket: |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the qubit's two basic states.

A qubit in superposition is written a|0⟩ + b|1⟩. The numbers a and b are amplitudes. When you look, you get 0 with chance a² and 1 with chance b², and the chances add to 1.

Example. |+⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩. Chance of 0 = 0.71² ≈ 0.5, chance of 1 ≈ 0.5: a fair coin.

|−⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ − 0.71|1⟩ gives the same 50/50 odds. The minus sign (the phase) does not change the odds here, but it makes |−⟩ a genuinely different state from |+⟩.

States as arrows on the Bloch circle

Each state can be drawn as an arrow: |0⟩ points up, |1⟩ down, |+⟩ right and |−⟩ left. Opposite arrows are states that can be told apart perfectly. In full 3D this circle becomes the Bloch sphere, whose third axis holds two more states, |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ (pointing toward and away from you).

Angles give odds. If two arrows are an angle θ apart, the chance one state "looks like" the other is cos²(θ/2). |0⟩ and |+⟩ are 90° apart: cos²(45°) = 0.5. |0⟩ and |1⟩ are 180° apart: cos²(90°) = 0.

Bases and measurement

A basis is a pair of opposite arrows you choose to measure along. The two used most are:

Z basis (+ shape)|0⟩ and |1⟩: up and down
X basis (× shape)|+⟩ and |−⟩: right and left

Measuring forces the qubit to pick one arrow of the basis, with the odds above, and the qubit then collapses: it becomes the arrow it picked and forgets its old state.

StateMeasured in ZMeasured in X
|0⟩always 0+ or − (50/50)
|+⟩0 or 1 (50/50)always +
Measuring in the wrong basis gives a random answer and destroys the original state. This one fact is the heart of quantum key distribution.

Two qubits and gates

Two qubits have four basic states: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. To combine two separate qubits, multiply them out like brackets in algebra (this is called the tensor product):

|+⟩|0⟩ = (0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩)|0⟩ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|10⟩

A gate is an operation that changes qubits. Gates are linear: they act on each part of a superposition separately and keep the amplitudes. The CNOT gate flips the second qubit when the first is 1, so the second becomes a ⊕ b:

In|00⟩|01⟩|10⟩|11⟩
Out|00⟩|01⟩|11⟩|10⟩

Entanglement and Bell states

Some two-qubit states can't be split into "first qubit times second qubit". They are entangled. The most famous is the Bell state:

Φ⁺ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩

Each qubit alone is a fair coin (50/50). But measured in the same basis, the two results always match: 00 or 11, never 01 or 10, even if the qubits are far apart.

There are four Bell states in all. Entangled pairs power the E91 protocol (6.2) and the Bell test (6.7).

5.2 Classical cryptographic models: how secrets are kept today

Cryptography is the science of keeping messages secret.

Alice, Bob and Eve

Every story in cryptography has the same three characters:

Alice wants to send a secret to Bob. Eve can see (and maybe change) everything that travels between them. The message before locking is the plaintext, the locked message is the ciphertext, and the secret number used to lock and unlock it is the key.

The method is public

In 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote down a rule still used today: assume Eve knows exactly how your lock works. Only the key is secret. Good locks are published and studied by everyone; if security depends on hiding the method, it breaks as soon as the method leaks.

Passive EveOnly listens and copies what passes by.
Active EveCan also block, change or fake messages, or pretend to be Bob ("man in the middle").

Symmetric (shared-key) cryptography

Alice and Bob use the same key to lock and unlock, like a house key both of them carry. A simple lock is XOR (⊕, 1.1): add the key bit by bit.

Example. Message 1011, key 0110.
Lock: 1011 ⊕ 0110 = 1101 (this is what Eve sees).
Unlock: 1101 ⊕ 0110 = 1011. XOR-ing the same key twice cancels out.

Real systems such as AES mix the bits far more thoroughly, but the idea is the same. Symmetric locks are fast, and Grover's algorithm only weakens them a little (1.1).

The catch: the key distribution problem. Before they can talk secretly, Alice and Bob must already share a key. How do they agree on one if Eve hears everything they say? This is the problem quantum cryptography solves.

Public-key cryptography

Here each person has two keys: a public key that locks (anyone may have it) and a private key that unlocks (kept secret). It is like a padlock you hand out open: anyone can snap it shut, only you can open it. RSA (1.1) works this way.

Toy RSA with tiny numbers. Bob picks primes 3 and 11, so N = 33. His public key is (N = 33, e = 3); his private key is d = 7.
Alice locks the message m = 4: 4³ = 64, and 64 mod 33 = 31. She sends 31.
Bob unlocks: 31⁷ mod 33 = 4. ✓ (31 is −2 on a 33-clock, and (−2)⁷ = −128, which wraps to 4.)
Eve sees N = 33 and 31. To find d she must factor 33 = 3 × 11, which is easy here but impossibly hard for a 600-digit N on an ordinary computer.

Two kinds of security

Computational security"Breaking it would take too long." Safe only while nobody has a faster method or a faster computer. RSA is in this group, and Shor's algorithm is exactly such a faster method.
Information-theoretic security"Breaking it is impossible, even with unlimited computing power, because the information simply isn't there." This is the gold standard, and the next section shows how it works.
Two answers to the quantum threat: post-quantum cryptography uses new kinds of maths that quantum computers can't easily break (still computational security). Quantum cryptography, the subject of this part, uses the laws of physics to reach information-theoretic security.

5.3 Information-theoretic security: unbreakable in principle

Is there any lock that no amount of computing power can ever open? Yes, and it is surprisingly simple.

The one-time pad

Use the XOR lock from 5.2 with a key that follows three strict rules:

1. Truly randomEvery key bit is a fair coin toss.
2. As long as the messageOne key bit for every message bit.
3. Used only onceThrown away after one message (hence "one-time").

In 1949 Claude Shannon proved that this one-time pad has perfect secrecy: the ciphertext tells Eve nothing at all about the message.

Why: every message is equally possible

Example. Eve sees the ciphertext 1101. Could the message be 1011? Yes, if the key was 0110. Could it be 0000? Yes, if the key was 1101. Could it be 1111? Yes, with key 0010. For every 4-bit message there is exactly one key that fits, and every key was equally likely. So all 16 messages are still equally likely: Eve has learned nothing.

Try the one-time pad

Tap the message bits to change them. Then play Eve: tap a guess, and see the key that would make your guess "correct".

Reuse and key supply

Never reuse the key. If two messages share a key, XOR-ing the two ciphertexts makes the key cancel out:

m₁ = 1010, m₂ = 0110, same key k = 1100.
c₁ = 1010 ⊕ 1100 = 0110 and c₂ = 0110 ⊕ 1100 = 1010.
Eve computes c₁ ⊕ c₂ = 0110 ⊕ 1010 = 1100 = m₁ ⊕ m₂. The key has vanished, and patterns in the messages start to leak.

So the one-time pad needs a huge supply of fresh, shared, random key. Physically carrying keys around is slow and risky. Quantum key distribution (6.1) is a way to grow such keys over a cable or through the air.

Measuring information in bits

Later sections need one more idea: how much Eve knows, counted in bits. Shannon's entropy measures how unpredictable something is:

Fair coin (50/50)Completely unpredictable: 1 bit of uncertainty.
Two-headed coinAlways heads: 0 bits. Nothing to learn.
Biased coin (97/3)Almost predictable: only about 0.19 bits.

For a yes/no event with chance p the formula is h(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p). Drag the slider to see it:

5.4 The no-cloning theorem: quantum states can't be copied

Copying is easy for ordinary data: you can photocopy a page or duplicate a file perfectly. In 1982 Wootters, Zurek and Dieks proved that no machine can make a perfect copy of an unknown qubit. This one fact is the foundation of quantum cryptography.

A copier that seems to work

Let's try to build a copier. It takes the qubit to copy and a blank qubit in |0⟩, and should output two copies. The CNOT gate (5.1) looks perfect:

Copy |0⟩CNOT |0⟩|0⟩ = |0⟩|0⟩ ✓ two copies of |0⟩
Copy |1⟩CNOT |1⟩|0⟩ = |1⟩|1⟩ ✓ two copies of |1⟩

It copies 0 and 1 perfectly. So far so good.

Where it fails: superpositions

Now feed it |+⟩ = 0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩. Gates act on each part of a superposition separately (they are linear, 5.1), so:

What the copier gives: 0.71|0⟩|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩|1⟩ = 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩. That's the Bell state Φ⁺ (5.1), an entangled pair!

What two copies would be: |+⟩|+⟩ = (0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩)(0.71|0⟩ + 0.71|1⟩) = 0.5|00⟩ + 0.5|01⟩ + 0.5|10⟩ + 0.5|11⟩ (tensor product, 5.1).

These are different: the copier has no |01⟩ or |10⟩ at all. The copy failed.

This isn't just a bad choice of copier. Any machine that copies |0⟩ and |1⟩ correctly must, by linearity, turn |+⟩ into 0.71|00⟩ + 0.71|11⟩, never into |+⟩|+⟩. And a machine tuned to copy |+⟩ and |−⟩ fails on |0⟩ and |1⟩. No machine copies them all.

What it means for security

Eve can't copy and keepShe can't make a private copy of Alice's qubit to study while forwarding the original untouched.
Looking disturbsMeasuring the qubit instead (5.1) collapses it, which changes it whenever she guesses the wrong basis. Those changes show up as errors.
Imperfect clonesThe best possible "approximate copier" makes copies that are only about 83% (5/6) faithful, so it also leaves traces.
No-cloning applies to unknown states. If you know a qubit is |0⟩ you can make as many |0⟩s as you like. Ordinary bits (which are always 0 or 1) can always be copied, which is why classical eavesdropping leaves no trace.
