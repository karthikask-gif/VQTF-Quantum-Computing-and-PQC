Part 6 · Quantum Key Distribution

Sharing a secret key with single photons, and catching anyone who listens.

6.1 Quantum key distribution: the BB84 protocol

Quantum key distribution (QKD) lets Alice and Bob grow a shared random key, ready for a one-time pad, so that any eavesdropping is detected. The first and most famous recipe is BB84 (Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard, 1984).

Qubits made of light

QKD sends qubits as single photons, the smallest possible pieces of light. A light wave wiggles in some direction as it travels; this direction is its polarization. Polarized sunglasses work by only letting one direction through.

BB84 uses four directions, grouped into two bases (5.1):

Measuring a photon in the same basis it was sent in gives the right bit every time. Measuring in the other basis gives a random result, 50/50, and destroys the original (5.1). Just like |0⟩ measured in the +/− basis.

The four steps of BB84

  1. Send. For each photon Alice picks a random bit and a random basis, and sends the matching polarization.
  2. Measure. Bob doesn't know her bases, so he picks a random basis for each photon and measures.
  3. Sift. Over an ordinary public channel they compare bases only (never the bits). They keep the bits where the bases matched (about half) and throw away the rest. The kept bits are the sifted key.
  4. Check. They publicly compare a small random sample of the sifted key. If too many differ, someone was listening (6.3, 6.4).
The public channel may be heard by Eve, but it must be authenticated: Bob must be sure he is really talking to Alice. A small shared starter key does this, so QKD actually "grows" a long key from a short one.

6.2 QKD protocols and their types

BB84 started a whole family of protocols. They differ in what is sent and where the security comes from.

Two big families

Prepare-and-measureAlice prepares each qubit and sends it; Bob measures it. Examples: BB84, B92, six-state.
Entanglement-basedA source in the middle sends one half of an entangled pair (5.1) to each of them. Examples: E91, BBM92.

B92 with just two states

Bennett showed in 1992 that two polarizations are enough, as long as they are not opposite: Alice sends → for 0 and ↗ for 1. Bob measures in a random basis and only keeps results that are impossible for one of the two states:

Bob measures in + and gets ↑. A → photon never gives ↑ in the + basis, so Alice must have sent ↗: the bit is 1, for sure.

Bob measures in × and gets ↖. A ↗ photon never gives ↖, so she sent →: the bit is 0.

Any other result (→ or ↗) could have come from either state, so it is discarded. Chance of a keepable result: ½ (right basis to rule one out) × ½ (the outcome lands on the "impossible" side) = 25%.

B92 is simpler to build but wastes more photons than BB84 (which keeps 50%).

Six-state protocol

This protocol adds a third basis, the circular one, matching the y-axis of the Bloch sphere (|+i⟩ and |−i⟩, 5.1). Only 1 in 3 bases match, so fewer bits survive sifting. In exchange Eve is easier to catch:

Intercept-resend (6.3) with three bases: Eve picks the wrong basis 2/3 of the time, and then Bob's bit is wrong half the time: 2/3 × 1/2 = 33% errors, compared with 25% in BB84.

E91 and BBM92: using entanglement

In E91 (Artur Ekert, 1991) a source sends each of them one half of a Bell pair Φ⁺ (5.1). When they happen to measure in the same direction, their results always match, so these become key bits. Results from different directions aren't wasted: they are used for a Bell test (6.7), which proves that nobody has tampered with the pairs.

BBM92 (1992) is the entangled version of BB84: both sides measure their half in + or × at random and sift just like BB84.

A neat bonus: with entanglement, the key doesn't exist anywhere until Alice and Bob measure. Not even the source knows it.

QKD is already in use: city fibre networks exist in several countries, and in 2017 the Chinese satellite Micius shared entangled photons between ground stations about 1,200 km apart.

6.3 Eavesdropping strategies: what Eve can try

Eve wants to learn the key without being noticed. Here are her main options, from simplest to most powerful, and why each one leaves traces.

Intercept and resend

Eve catches each photon, measures it in a random basis, and sends Bob a new photon matching what she saw. Follow one photon from the sifted key (where Alice and Bob used the same basis):

The numbers. Chance of an error = ½ (Eve picks the wrong basis) × ½ (Bob's measurement then lands on the wrong bit) = ¼ = 25%. In a 1,000-bit sifted key, about 250 bits would differ. Meanwhile Eve knows only the bits where she happened to pick the right basis: about half.

Photon-number splitting

Real QKD often uses a very dim laser instead of a perfect single-photon source. Now and then a pulse contains two photons with the same polarization. Eve can split one off, keep it, and let the other reach Bob untouched: no errors at all.

How often? With an average of 0.1 photons per pulse, the laws of chance (a Poisson distribution) give: 90.5% of pulses are empty, 9.05% hold one photon, and 0.47% hold two or more. So among pulses that carry anything, about 1 in 20 can be split.

The fix: decoy states. Alice randomly mixes in pulses of different brightness. Eve can't tell decoys from real pulses, so if she favours multi-photon pulses, the detection rates of the decoys come out wrong and she is caught.

Cloning and smarter attacks

Approximate cloningEve uses the best possible imperfect copier (5.4). She keeps one copy and forwards the other, but the forwarded copy is only about 83% faithful, causing errors.
Individual attacksEve probes each photon on its own and measures her probe immediately (intercept-resend is one).
Collective attacksEve attaches a quantum probe to each photon, stores all probes in a quantum memory, and measures them together after hearing the public discussion.
Coherent attacksThe most general: anything quantum physics allows, on all photons together. Modern security proofs cover even these.
The key fact behind every security proof: information gained by Eve always costs disturbance. The more she learns, the higher the error rate Alice and Bob see.

Attacks around the physics

Man in the middleEve pretends to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob. Stopped by authenticating the public channel (6.1).
Side channels and hackingAttacking the real equipment: blinding Bob's detectors with bright light, or shining light into Alice's device to read its settings (a "Trojan horse"). Stopped by careful engineering, and by MDI and device-independent QKD (6.2).

6.4 Error rate estimation: is anyone listening?

After sifting, Alice and Bob must find out how many of their bits disagree. That number is their eavesdropping alarm.

The quantum bit error rate (QBER)

The QBER is the fraction of sifted bits where Bob's bit differs from Alice's:

QBER = number of mismatched bits ÷ number of bits compared

To estimate it they publicly compare a random sample of the sifted key, then throw those bits away (Eve heard them).

Example. 1,000 sifted bits. They compare 100 bits chosen at random and find 3 that differ. Estimated QBER = 3 ÷ 100 = 3%. The remaining 900 bits are kept for the key.

Even with no Eve, real equipment gives a few percent of errors from noise in the fibre and imperfect detectors. Alice and Bob can't tell noise from Eve, so they play safe: every error is assumed to be Eve's work.

How much can a sample be trusted?

A sample is like a poll: it gives an estimate, not the exact value. The bigger the sample, the closer the estimate. A rough guide for the "wobble" is √(Q × (1 − Q) ÷ n):

With Q = 3% and n = 100 compared bits: √(0.03 × 0.97 ÷ 100) ≈ 1.7%, so the true rate is likely between about 0% and 6.4% (within two wobbles). With n = 10,000 the wobble is just 0.17%.

Try sampling yourself. Set the true error rate (normally unknown), then sample.

Continue or abort?

QBER below about 11%Keep going. Error correction (6.5) and privacy amplification (6.6) can still produce a key that is perfectly secret.
QBER above about 11%Abort. Eve may know too much, and no secret key can be extracted. Try again later or use another line.

Where 11% comes from is shown in 6.6. For comparison: a full intercept-resend attack causes 25%, far above the limit.

6.5 Information reconciliation: fixing the errors

If the QBER is acceptable, Alice's and Bob's keys are almost the same. Information reconciliation finds and fixes the few differences by talking over the public channel, without revealing the key itself.

Order of the steps: reconciliation comes before privacy amplification, because the error-fixing chat leaks a little information that must be removed afterwards.

Parity checks

The parity of a block of bits is whether it has an even (0) or odd (1) number of 1s.

Example. Block 1011 has three 1s: odd, so parity = 1. Block 1001 has two: even, parity = 0.
If Alice's and Bob's blocks have different parities, the blocks must differ in an odd number of places (most likely one).

Worked example, binary search

Alice has 10110010 and Bob has 10100010 (one error, which they don't yet know about).

1. Whole block: Alice's key has four 1s (even, parity 0). Bob's has three 1s (odd, parity 1). Different, so there is an error somewhere.

2. Split in half. First halves: Alice 1011 (parity 1), Bob 1010 (parity 0). Different, so the error is in the first half.

3. Split again. Alice 10 (parity 1), Bob 10 (parity 1). Same, so the error is in the other quarter: Alice 11, Bob 10.

4. Last split. Bit 3: both 1, same. So the error is bit 4. Bob flips it: 10110010. ✓

Total parities announced: 4 (one per step).

Each announced parity is a bit of information Eve also hears. Finding one error among 2ᵏ bits takes about k parities.

Hunt the error

Bob's 16-bit key has one hidden error. Press Compare parity to halve the search area each time.

Cascade and the cost of talking

A single parity can't spot two errors in the same block (two flips leave the parity unchanged). The Cascade protocol fixes this by shuffling the bits and repeating the search several times with different block sizes. Modern systems often use error-correcting codes called LDPC codes instead, which work the same way in spirit.

The minimum cost. Shannon showed that fixing errors at rate Q needs at least h(Q) public bits per key bit (5.3). With 900 bits at Q = 3%: h(0.03) ≈ 0.194, so at least 900 × 0.194 ≈ 175 bits leak to Eve. Real methods leak a bit more.

6.6 Privacy amplification: squeezing out Eve's knowledge

Alice and Bob now share identical keys, but Eve may know part of them: from eavesdropping (counted by the QBER) and from the parities in reconciliation. Privacy amplification turns this long, partly-known key into a shorter key that Eve knows nothing about.

The idea with two bits

Suppose the key is two bits x₁x₂ and Eve knows x₁ but not x₂. Alice and Bob replace the two bits with one: x₁ ⊕ x₂.

Since x₂ is a fair coin to Eve, the new bit is a fair coin too. Eve's knowledge of x₁ has become useless. The price: the key got shorter.

Hashing a whole key

For long keys, Alice and Bob agree publicly on a random hash: a mixing rule that turns many bits into fewer, where every output bit depends on many input bits. A simple kind is a table of random 0s and 1s (called a matrix): each output bit is the parity of the key bits picked out by one row.

Key K = 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 0 (8 bits) → 4-bit final key.

Row 1 = 1 1 0 1 0 0 1 0 picks bits 1, 2, 4, 7 = 1, 0, 1, 0: two 1s, even → 0

Row 2 = 1 0 1 1 0 0 0 1 picks bits 1, 3, 4, 8 = 1, 1, 1, 0: three 1s, odd → 1

Row 3 = 0 1 0 0 1 1 1 0 picks bits 2, 5, 6, 7 = 0, 0, 1, 0: one 1, odd → 1

Row 4 = 0 0 1 0 0 1 1 1 picks bits 3, 6, 7, 8 = 1, 1, 0, 0: two 1s, even → 0

Final key = 0110. If Eve knew up to about 4 of the 8 bits, she knows essentially nothing about these 4.

The rule: shrink the key by at least as many bits as Eve might know, plus a small safety margin.

How long is the final key?

For BB84, with error rate Q, Eve's possible knowledge is about h(Q) bits per key bit, and reconciliation leaks another h(Q). So the fraction of secret bits left is:

secret key fraction r = 1 − 2·h(Q)

Q = 3%: h = 0.194, so r = 1 − 0.388 = 0.61. 61% of the bits survive.
Q = 11%: h ≈ 0.50, so r ≈ 1 − 1.0 = 0. Nothing survives. That is where the 11% limit (6.4) comes from.

6.7 Bell inequalities in cryptographic security

In 1964 John Bell found a test that tells true entanglement apart from any "pre-planned" imitation. In cryptography it becomes a lie detector: it proves that nobody, not even the maker of the equipment, could know the key.

The question Bell asked

Measure a Bell pair (5.1) and the results are perfectly linked. Could the two particles simply have agreed on their answers in advance, like twins with a shared script? Such pre-planned answers are called local hidden variables. Einstein suspected something like this. Bell showed it can be tested.

If answers were pre-planned, Eve could have planned them too, or could know the plan. So ruling out pre-planned answers also rules out Eve knowing the key.

The CHSH game

The most-used form of Bell's test is the CHSH inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969). Each round:

  1. Alice measures her particle in one of two directions, a or a′, chosen at random. Bob does the same with b or b′.
  2. Each result is +1 or −1.
  3. Over many rounds they compute the correlation E for each pair of settings: the average of (Alice's result × Bob's result). E = +1 means always equal, −1 always opposite, 0 no link.

Then they combine the four correlations:

S = E(a,b) + E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) − E(a′,b′)

Pre-planned answers can't beat 2

Suppose each particle carries fixed answers A, A′ (for Alice's two settings) and B, B′ (for Bob's), each +1 or −1. For one pair:

A·B + A·B′ + A′·B − A′·B′ = A·(B + B′) + A′·(B − B′)

Either B = B′, so (B + B′) = ±2 and (B − B′) = 0, or B = −B′, so (B + B′) = 0 and (B − B′) = ±2.

Either way the total is ±2. Example: A = A′ = B = B′ = +1 gives 1 + 1 + 1 − 1 = 2.

Averaging over many pairs can't beat the largest single value, so S ≤ 2 for any pre-planned scheme. This is Bell's inequality.

Quantum pairs reach 2.83

For the Bell pair Φ⁺, measured along directions at angles on the Bloch circle (5.1), the correlation is E = cos(angle between the directions). Choose a = 0°, a′ = 90°, b = 45°, b′ = −45°:

E(a,b) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a,b′) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a′,b) = cos 45° = 0.707

E(a′,b′) = cos 135° = −0.707

S = 0.707 + 0.707 + 0.707 − (−0.707) = 2.83 (= 2√2)

2.83 is bigger than 2: no pre-planned script can produce these results. This has been confirmed in many experiments, including "loophole-free" tests in 2015, and earned the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics (Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger).

Why it protects the key

Entanglement is monogamousIf Alice's and Bob's particles are maximally linked (S ≈ 2.83), there is no link left over for a third party. Eve can't be correlated with their results.
Eve pushes S downIf Eve measures the particles on their way (intercept-resend), she breaks the entanglement and S falls to about 1.41, well below 2.
Trust no deviceDevice-independent QKD checks only that S > 2. Even if the equipment came from Eve's factory, a high S proves the key is private.

QKD protects key distribution; the key is then used in a one-time pad or a strong symmetric cipher such as AES. Its limits today are distance (photons get lost in fibre, so links reach a few hundred kilometres without trusted relay stations), cost, and the need for authentication. That is why post-quantum cryptography and QKD are seen as partners rather than rivals.
