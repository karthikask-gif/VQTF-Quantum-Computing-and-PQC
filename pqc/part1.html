Part 1 · Foundations

Why quantum computers threaten today's encryption, and what PQC is.

1.1 Background: what you need first

This course is about protecting data from quantum computers. Before we start, here are the few ideas it builds on, each in plain words with a small example.

Locks and keys

Cryptography is the science of keeping messages secret and genuine. It comes in two kinds.

SymmetricOne shared secret key both locks and unlocks. Fast, used for the bulk of the data. Example: AES.
Public-keyEach person has a public key (an open padlock anyone may use) and a private key (the only key that opens it). It lets strangers set up a shared key and sign messages. Examples: RSA, Diffie–Hellman.
RSA in one line. Multiplying is easy: 3 × 11 = 33. Going backward, "which two primes make 33?", is factoring. For 33 it is instant, but for a number with 600 digits no ordinary computer can do it. RSA publishes the big product and keeps the two primes secret.

Hash functions: fingerprints of data

A hash function turns any input into a short fixed-size "fingerprint". It is easy to compute and practically impossible to reverse, and a tiny change in the input changes the whole output.

SHA-256("hello") = 2cf24dba5fb0a30e…

SHA-256("hellp") = fdd7585e08c4e2af…

One letter changed, a completely different fingerprint.

Clock arithmetic and XOR

mod means "the remainder after dividing", like a clock that wraps around. On a 12-hour clock, 14 o'clock is 2 o'clock: 14 mod 12 = 2.

Example. 17 mod 5: walk 17 steps around a 5-hour clock. Three full turns use 15 steps, 2 are left, so 17 mod 5 = 2.

XOR (written ⊕) compares two bits: the answer is 1 when they differ and 0 when they match. It is how bits are "added on a 2-hour clock".

Quantum computers in brief

An ordinary computer stores bits, each 0 or 1, like a coin lying heads or tails. A quantum computer stores qubits, which can be in a blend of 0 and 1 at once (a superposition), like a spinning coin.

This does not make it faster at everything. It is faster only for problems where a clever quantum algorithm can make the wrong answers cancel out. Two such algorithms matter for security: Shor's and Grover's.

Shor's algorithm

Shor's algorithm finds the period of powers on a clock: how often the pattern repeats. Knowing the period cracks factoring, and also the discrete logarithm problem behind Diffie–Hellman (explained in 1.2). Here is the classical half of the idea with N = 15.

2⁰→12¹→22²→42³→82⁴→12⁵→22⁶→42⁷→8

Powers of 2 mod 15 repeat every 4 steps (same colours), so the period is r = 4.

Take 2^(r/2) = 2² = 4. Then 4 − 1 = 3 and 4 + 1 = 5, and indeed 15 = 3 × 5.

For a 600-digit number the pattern is far too long to list, but a large quantum computer finds the period quickly. That breaks RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography.

Grover's algorithm

Grover's algorithm searches a list of N possibilities in about √N steps instead of N. Against a secret key this halves the key's strength in bits.

KeyOrdinary guessingWith Grover
AES-1282¹²⁸ tries√2¹²⁸ = 2⁶⁴ steps (too weak)
AES-2562²⁵⁶ tries2¹²⁸ steps (still safe)
The fix for Grover is easy: double the key length. The fix for Shor is not: public-key cryptography needs entirely new maths. That is what this course is about.

Harvest now, decrypt later

An attacker can record encrypted traffic today, store it, and decrypt it years from now once a big quantum computer exists. So data that must stay secret for a long time is already at risk, even though no such computer exists yet.

Numerical example. A medical record encrypted in 2026 must stay private for 15 years, until 2041. If a large quantum computer appears in 2035, the stored copy is readable for the last 2041 − 2035 = 6 years of its secret life. Mosca's rule (1.2) turns this into a simple test.

1.2 The quantum threat: what breaks and when

Almost every secure website, bank app and messaging service relies on two kinds of public-key maths. Shor's algorithm (1.1) breaks both.

Factoring (RSA)

RSA's security rests on factoring being hard (1.1). Shor's algorithm finds the factors quickly, so a large enough quantum computer breaks RSA outright.

RSA, step by step (tiny numbers)

Key generation. Pick two secret primes p = 3 and q = 11. Public n = p × q = 33. Compute φ = (p − 1)(q − 1) = 2 × 10 = 20.

Choose the public exponent e = 3. The private exponent d undoes it: e × d leaves remainder 1 when divided by 20. d = 7, because 3 × 7 = 21 = 20 + 1.

Public key: (n = 33, e = 3). Private key: d = 7.

Encrypt the message m = 4: c = 4³ mod 33 = 64 mod 33 = 31.

Decrypt: 31⁷ mod 33 = 4. The message is back.

How Shor breaks it. Eve sees n = 33 and e = 3. If she can factor 33 = 3 × 11, she computes φ = 20 and then d = 7 exactly as Alice did. Factoring is the only hard step, and Shor's algorithm (1.1) makes it easy.

Discrete logarithms (Diffie–Hellman, ECC)

The other big family uses powers on a clock (mod, 1.1). Going forward is easy: 3⁴ mod 17 = 81 mod 17 = 13. Going backward ("which power of 3 gives 13?") is the discrete logarithm problem, and it is very hard for big numbers.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange, step by step

This is how two strangers agree on a secret key in public. Public numbers: clock size p = 23 and base g = 5.

Alice secretly picks a = 6 and sends A = 5⁶ mod 23 = 8.

Bob secretly picks b = 15 and sends B = 5¹⁵ mod 23 = 19.

Alice computes B⁶ = 19⁶ mod 23 = 2. Bob computes A¹⁵ = 8¹⁵ mod 23 = 2. Both have the shared secret 2.

Eve sees 23, 5, 8 and 19. To get the secret she must find a = 6 from 5ᵃ mod 23 = 8: a discrete logarithm.

Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) plays the same game, but the "clock" is replaced by points on a curve (shapes like y² = x³ + ax + b), which gives the same security with much smaller keys. Shor's algorithm solves discrete logarithms too, so Diffie–Hellman and ECC fall as well.

Elliptic curves, step by step

Take the curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2 on a clock of size 17 (every number is taken mod 17). It has just 18 points, the dots below. Points can be "added" with a fixed rule, so starting from a base point G = (5, 1) we can hop to 2G, 3G and so on.

Doubling G, worked out

To double G = (5, 1), find the slope (steepness) of the curve at G: λ = (3x² + 2) ÷ (2y) = (3·25 + 2) ÷ 2 = 77 ÷ 2. Mod 17: 77 mod 17 = 9, and "÷ 2" means "× 9" (because 2 × 9 = 18 = 1 mod 17). So λ = 9 × 9 = 81 mod 17 = 13.

x = λ² − 2·5 = 169 − 10 = 159 mod 17 = 6. y = λ(5 − 6) − 1 = −14 mod 17 = 3. So 2G = (6, 3).

ECC key exchange

Alice picks secret a = 3 and sends A = 3G = (10, 6). Bob picks secret b = 5 and sends B = 5G = (9, 16).

Alice computes 3·B = 15G, Bob computes 5·A = 15G. Both get (3, 16).

Eve sees G, (10, 6) and (9, 16). To find a she must solve "how many hops from G reach (10, 6)?", the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm. With 19 hops she can just count; real curves have about 2²⁵⁶ points.

Why Shor still wins

After 19 hops the pattern comes back to the start (19G is the curve's "zero point"). Powers on a clock repeat in the same way, for example 3ˣ mod 7:

3¹→33²→23³→63⁴→43⁵→53⁶→13⁷→3

Any repeating pattern has a hidden period, and finding periods is exactly what Shor's algorithm does fast. That is why both Diffie–Hellman and ECC fall.

What survives

So the urgent job is to replace public-key cryptography: key exchange and digital signatures.

Mosca's rule: why act now

Michele Mosca gave a simple test. Let

xHow many years your data must stay secret.
yHow many years it takes to switch to new cryptography.
zHow many years until a quantum computer can break today's locks.

If x + y > z, you are already too late: secrets recorded today ("harvest now, decrypt later", 1.1) will be readable while they still matter.

Example. Medical records must stay private for x = 25 years, and switching takes y = 5 years. If a quantum computer arrives in z = 15 years, then 25 + 5 = 30 > 15: the switch should have started long ago.

1.3 PQC basics: new hard problems

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) means public-key methods that run on ordinary computers but rest on maths problems that are believed to be hard even for quantum computers. No special hardware is needed: it is just new software.

Two jobs to replace

Key exchange: a KEMA key encapsulation mechanism lets two parties agree on a shared secret key (then used with AES). It replaces Diffie–Hellman and RSA key exchange.
Digital signaturesA signature proves who wrote a message and that it wasn't changed, like a tamper-proof wax seal. It replaces RSA and ECDSA signatures.

How a KEM works

The three steps are called KeyGen (Bob makes a key pair), Encaps (Alice makes a secret and its capsule, the ciphertext) and Decaps (Bob opens it).

Five families of hard problems

LatticesFinding the nearest point in a huge multi-dimensional grid (2.1 to 2.4). The main family.
Hash functionsReversing a fingerprint (3.1). The most conservative choice.
Error-correcting codesFixing errors in a scrambled code (3.2). Studied since 1978.
Multivariate equationsSolving many quadratic equations at once (3.3).
IsogeniesFinding paths between elliptic curves (3.3). One scheme was broken in 2022.
None of these problems has a known fast quantum algorithm. Shor's trick needs a hidden period (1.1), and these problems don't have one.

Hard problems at a glance

Every public-key scheme is a one-way street: easy to go forward, hard to go back unless you hold a secret shortcut (the private key).

Compare with RSA: forward 3 × 11 = 33 is easy, backward 33 → 3 × 11 is hard for normal computers but easy for Shor. The rows above have no known shortcut, quantum or classical.

Security levels

Each scheme comes in sizes matched to how hard AES is to break: Level 1 ≈ AES-128, Level 3 ≈ AES-192, Level 5 ≈ AES-256. Bigger level, bigger keys, more safety margin.
